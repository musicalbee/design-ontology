Katazome and Ise katagami stencil print

print · c.1600–1960s · also: Katazome, 型染, Ise katagami, 伊勢型紙, Japanese paper stencil dyeing

A stencil is cut in layers of mulberry paper, and rice-paste resist is spread through it onto cloth. The cloth is then dyed. The result is a repeat pattern with sharp white edges and flat colour.

Core thesis

A pattern cut once in paper can be printed a thousand times as a sharp white shape on dyed cloth, if the stencil holds its islands with thin bridges.

Design principles

  • Cut the pattern in 2–3 layers of washi that are pasted with persimmon tannin (kakishibu), so the stencil stays stiff and wet-proof.
  • Keep each cut line 0.1–0.5 mm wide, and keep at least 0.5 mm of paper between two cuts.
  • Hold each free island of paper with a thin bridge, a tie bar, or with a net of silk thread between the layers.
  • Spread the rice-paste resist through the stencil in one pass of a spatula, and let it dry before you dye.
  • Dye the cloth after the resist dries. The resist stays white, and the dye fills the rest as a flat colour.
  • Repeat the pattern on the cloth, and join each repeat so the seam does not show.
  • Choose a small, fine motif: a wave, a plant, a crest, a bird. Allow a pictorial scene only in mingei work.

Anti-patterns

  • A soft gradient or a tonal shade
  • Halftone dots or a photograph
  • A woodgrain texture or a thick outline
  • A pattern whose islands float with no bridge
  • A three-dimensional effect

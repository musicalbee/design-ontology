The oner

film · 1948–today · also: One-take film, Long continuous take, Plan-séquence

A scene or a whole film in one unbroken shot. The camera follows people through rooms and streets, and the cut is hidden or absent. Time is real, and the crew must be perfect.

Core thesis

One unbroken shot holds the viewer in real time and space, so the viewer cannot look away and feels each mistake and each turn as the actors do.

Design principles

  • Plan the shot on a floor plan, and rehearse it for 2 to 8 weeks.
  • Run the shot for 2 to 12 min for a scene, or 90 min or more for a feature film.
  • Keep the camera in motion at 0.5 to 1.5 m/s, with a steadicam or a rig, and pass it between operators if needed.
  • Hide any cut in a dark door, a pan to a wall or a passing body, if the film is faked as one take.
  • Block each actor with a mark and a cue, and keep 3 to 5 planes of action in the frame.
  • Light the whole set at one level of 3 to 5 stops range so the camera can turn 360°.
  • Keep the sound as one continuous mix, and record the real room.

Anti-patterns

  • A cut to cover a mistake
  • A cutaway to a reaction face
  • A rapid montage
  • A tidy shot–reverse-shot talk
  • A fixed set with one angle
  • A score that restarts at each cut

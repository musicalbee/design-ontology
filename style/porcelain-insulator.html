Porcelain insulator

ceramics · 1850 to now · also: Electrical porcelain, Ceramic line insulator, Telegraph and power insulator

Glazed porcelain that holds a live wire away from a pole or a tower. The shape follows the electric field and the rain: a stack of skirts, a brown or grey glaze, and a cement joint to a metal cap.

Core thesis

A wire needs a dry, hard, glazed surface between it and the earth, so the clay body is dense and the shape is a stack of rain skirts.

Design principles

  • Fire a dense body with water absorption below 0.5 %, so that no moisture enters the wall.
  • Glaze every outer surface 0.3 to 0.5 mm thick, in brown or light grey, to shed rain and dirt.
  • Shape the outer face as 2 to 4 skirts that overhang, so that the inner face stays dry in rain.
  • Grow the creepage path with the voltage: about 20 to 25 mm of surface for each kV of line voltage.
  • Leave a ring of the glaze off the cement seat and coat it with sand, so that the cement holds.
  • Make a suspension disc 146 mm across and 254 mm apart in a string, so that parts fit each other.
  • Stamp the maker, the year and the load on the shoulder in raised or incised letters.

Anti-patterns

  • A thin white pot with a gold line and a floral print
  • A porous body that shows a bare, rough surface under the glaze
  • A single smooth cylinder with no skirt to break the water film
  • Bright colour glaze that hides tracking marks and dirt
  • A hand-painted pattern on the skirt

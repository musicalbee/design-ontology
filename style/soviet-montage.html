Soviet montage

film · 1924–1930 · also: Montage theory, Intellectual montage, Kino-Eye

A cinema made in the cutting room. Two shots meet and a third meaning appears. Fast cuts, steep angles and typed faces drive the argument of the film.

Core thesis

Meaning comes from the collision of shots, not from the content of a single shot, so the edit is the author of the film.

Design principles

  • Cut at an average shot length of 2 to 3 s (Man with a Movie Camera 2.1 s, Battleship Potemkin 2.8 s on Cinemetrics) and shorten the shots toward a climax.
  • Join two shots so that their collision makes a third idea: pair the image with its opposite and hold each for 1 to 3 s.
  • Choose the cut by one of five methods: metric (fixed frame count), rhythmic, tonal, overtonal or intellectual, and keep one method for each sequence.
  • Shoot from low or high angles of 20° to 45° off level, with diagonals that cross the 1.33:1 frame, and fill the frame with a type, not an individual.
  • Build a sequence from 8 to 20 shots of a single action seen from many angles, and cut on the peak of the movement.
  • Use hard daylight on location at high contrast, and print on black and white stock with deep blacks.
  • Limit the intertitles to 2 to 4 words in a large heavy sans-serif face, 1 to 2 s on screen.

Anti-patterns

  • Long takes of over 8 s with no inner cut
  • Invisible continuity cuts that hide the edit
  • A star actor as a psychological individual
  • Eye-level, centred, symmetrical coverage
  • Studio sets built for naturalistic depth
  • Soft, diffused light
  • A story with no social class or crowd

Copper engraving

print · 1430s–1800 · also: Line engraving, Burin engraving, Kupferstich, Intaglio engraving

A picture cut into a copper plate with a burin, inked into the grooves and printed under great pressure. Schongauer and Dürer made it an art; Goltzius made the swelling line a show of skill; for three centuries it reproduced paintings and maps.

Core thesis

A burin line that swells and tapers in polished copper gives the finest and most even tone that a hand can make.

Design principles

  • Build every tone from lines that swell in the middle and taper at both ends.
  • Lay the lines in systems: parallel lines for a half tone, a second set crossed at 30–45° for the shadow, and dots in the gaps (dot-and-lozenge) for the deepest dark.
  • Turn each line round the form, like the lines of a contour map, so the swell shows the light.
  • Keep the line pitch at 80–150 a inch; the eye at reading distance sees a grey, not lines.
  • Leave a clear plate mark 10–20 mm outside the image, with the title and the names of the designer and the engraver in the margin.
  • Print in one black or dark brown ink on dampened paper.

Anti-patterns

  • Lines of one even width
  • Hatching that does not follow the form
  • A soft, grained tone with no lines
  • A coloured fill or a flat area of solid ink
  • Trimmed margins that cut off the plate mark

Creamware, neoclassical

ceramics · 1762–1830 · also: Queen's ware, Wedgwood creamware, Leeds creamware

Cream-coloured lead-glazed earthenware that is light, cheap and strong. Potters in Staffordshire and Leeds make plain, classical forms with a feather edge or pierced work, and sell it to every table in Europe.

Core thesis

A light cream body and a clear glaze, in a plain classical form, outsell both porcelain and delftware, because the whole service is cheap, even and easy to match.

Design principles

  • Fire a body of flint and white clay to a cream earthenware at 1100 °C.
  • Dip the piece in a clear lead glaze that fires to a pale yellow-cream gloss.
  • Keep the form plain and balanced: a circle, a straight rim, a loop handle.
  • Press or mould the edge with a feather or a shell, in a regular pattern of fine lines.
  • Pierce the wall with a punch for openwork baskets, in rows of small holes 3 to 4 mm wide.
  • Print or paint a pattern in a narrow band, and leave most of the surface plain.
  • Make the service in 50 or more shapes, so that a buyer can order any piece again.

Anti-patterns

  • A white opaque tin glaze on a buff body
  • A modelled baroque relief with applied flowers
  • A deep ground colour with gold reserves
  • A thick, hand-painted, uneven rim

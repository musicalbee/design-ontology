Slow cinema

film · 1994–today · also: Contemplative cinema, Long-take cinema

A cinema of duration. Few cuts, a still or slow camera, ordinary tasks shown in real time and little plot. The viewer's attention does the work that the cut usually does.

Core thesis

Time is the material of film, so a shot is held until the viewer sees the place and the person and feels the hours pass.

Design principles

  • Hold each shot for 30 to 180 s and cut at an average shot length of 30 s or more, with a whole scene in one take.
  • Lock the camera on a tripod, or move it at a walking pace of 0.2 to 0.5 m/s along a path that the person sets.
  • Frame in a wide shot with 40% to 70% of the picture left to a landscape, a room or a sky, and keep the person at 10% to 30% of the height.
  • Use the available light, a key-to-fill ratio of 2:1 or lower, with long daylight shots from dawn to dusk.
  • Shoot ordinary work, such as a walk, a meal or a wait, in real time and leave no plot reason for it.
  • Record the sound of the place, with wind, rain and an engine, at a level that carries the scene, and use little music or none.
  • Keep the dialogue under 10 lines in a scene and let a pause run 5 to 15 s.

Anti-patterns

  • Fast cutting under 5 s average shot length
  • A score that tells the viewer how to feel
  • A shot and reverse shot coverage of each line
  • An expository line that explains the plot
  • A hero with a clear goal and a finish
  • A camera move for spectacle
  • A cutaway that releases the tension of a long wait

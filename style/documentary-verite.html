Direct cinema and vérité

film · 1960–1975 · also: Cinéma vérité, Direct cinema, Observational documentary

A handheld 16mm camera and a synchronised tape recorder stay with real people. No script, no set and no lamps. The camera sits in the room and waits. The picture is rough, and the sound is complete.

Core thesis

Light gear and sync sound let the camera watch people in real time, so the film is made of what happens and not of what is staged.

Design principles

  • Shoot with a handheld 16mm camera and a synchronised tape recorder, with a crew of 2 to 3 people.
  • Use the available light only, with a fast stock pushed 1 to 2 stops, and let a window blow out to white.
  • Frame in 1.33:1 or 1.37:1 at eye level, with a 10mm to 25mm zoom or a 9.5mm to 25mm lens, and let the subject move out of focus for 1 to 3 s.
  • Hold a take of 20 to 120 s and cut at an average shot length of 8 to 15 s, with no cutaway to hide a seam.
  • Never stage or ask for a repeat; follow the event at 1 to 1.5 m/s and arrive after it begins.
  • Record the real sound of the room and let people talk over each other; leave the voice-over out or limit it to 5% of the runtime.
  • Interview in the street or at home, and keep the question and the interviewer's voice in the cut.

Anti-patterns

  • A staged, re-enacted scene
  • A voice-of-God narration over every shot
  • Studio lighting with a key and a fill
  • A tripod and a polished dolly move
  • A score laid over the events
  • A tidy interview in a set
  • A cutaway that hides a jump

Jōmon pottery

ceramics · c. 14000–300 BC · also: Jomon ware, Cord-marked pottery, Flame-style pottery

The oldest pottery of Japan. Hunter-gatherers coiled a coarse clay into deep pots, pressed twisted cord into the wet wall, and fired the pots in an open bonfire.

Core thesis

A pot is a coiled and fired form that carries the marks of its making: the cord, the cut and the clay itself are the ornament.

Design principles

  • Build the pot from clay coils 15 to 25 mm thick and smooth only the inside.
  • Roll twisted cord across the wet outer wall to leave a dense, even texture.
  • Keep the pot deep: the height is one to two times the mouth width.
  • Fire the pot in an open bonfire at 600 to 900 °C, with no kiln and no glaze.
  • Raise the rim of a ceremonial pot into a crown of clay loops, handles and spikes.
  • Divide the surface into bands: cord-marked, incised, and plain zones that alternate.
  • Leave the clay body in its fired colour, from buff to dark brown, with black fire clouds.

Anti-patterns

  • A glossy glaze or any liquid surface on the wall
  • A smooth thrown wall with the rings of the wheel
  • A flat base with a turned foot ring
  • Thin even walls less than 5 mm thick
  • Painted pictures of people or animals in colour

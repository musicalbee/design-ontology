Swiss International Style

graphic movement · 1950s–1970s · also: International Typographic Style, Swiss Style, Swiss Grid

Asymmetric layouts on a mathematical grid, one neo-grotesque sans serif, flush-left text and objective photography. It came out of Zurich and Basel in the 1950s and became the default language of corporate modernism.

Core thesis

Design is a neutral carrier of information, and a mathematical grid makes that neutrality visible.

Design principles

  • Build every layout on a modular grid of 6 or 12 columns and align every element to a column edge and a baseline.
  • Use one sans-serif family for the whole piece, and make hierarchy with size and weight only.
  • Set all text flush left with a ragged right edge.
  • Compose asymmetrically: put the main mass off centre and balance it with empty space.
  • Use at most one accent colour in addition to black and white, and use it on less than 10% of the surface.
  • Jump between type sizes by a ratio of 1.5 or more, so that each level reads as a different level.
  • Prefer photography and plain diagrams to illustration, and crop photographs to grid modules.

Anti-patterns

  • Centred body text or centred multi-line headlines
  • Drop shadows, gradients, glows or any simulated depth
  • Rounded corners on containers, images or buttons
  • A second typeface, script fonts or decorative display fonts
  • Decorative illustration, ornaments, emoji or stock icons used as filler
  • Elements placed by eye that do not sit on a grid line
  • Justified text with uneven word spacing

Palette

  • Paper #FFFFFF background
  • Ink #111111 text and rules
  • Swiss Red #E30613 single accent
  • Grey #5E5E5E secondary text
  • Fog #F2F2F0 alternate surface

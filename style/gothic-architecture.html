Gothic

architecture · 13th–early 16th century · also: High Gothic, Rayonnant, Perpendicular

Pointed arches, ribbed vaults and flying buttresses turn the stone wall into a skeleton, and the freed walls become coloured glass. Chartres, Reims and Amiens set the height record that every later cathedral tried to beat.

Core thesis

A building is a stone skeleton that lifts the eye to heaven: the thrust of each vault is caught by a buttress, so that walls can dissolve into light.

Design principles

  • Draw every opening as a pointed arch, with a rise of 1.0 to 1.5 times the span, so that the thrust goes down and not out.
  • Cover each bay with a rib vault, and carry the ribs down on clustered shafts so that the vault line reaches the floor.
  • Catch the thrust of the high vault with flying buttresses outside the aisle roof, at about 45° to the wall.
  • Raise the nave to a height of 2 to 3.5 times its width, from 30 to 48m under the vault, in three tiers: arcade, triforium, clerestory.
  • Give the upper wall to glass: windows fill 60 to 80% of the clerestory wall, divided by stone tracery.
  • Repeat the bay at 5 to 7m along the whole nave, so that the eye is led to the altar by the rhythm of the piers.

Anti-patterns

  • Round arches and heavy plain walls with small openings
  • Flat ceilings or timber trusses left open over the nave
  • Classical orders, pediments or symmetrical Roman porticos
  • Wide, low proportions that stop the vertical lift
  • Smooth, bare stone with no moulding or tracery
  • Clear plain glass with no tracery or colour

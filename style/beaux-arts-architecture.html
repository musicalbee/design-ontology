Beaux-Arts

architecture · 1860s–1910s · also: Second Empire, Academic classicism, American Renaissance

A grand classical system taught at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris: a clear plan on axes, a rich sculpted front with paired columns and a mansard roof. It made the opera house, the station and the city hall into public palaces.

Core thesis

A great building is planned first: the parti is a clear sequence of axes, rooms and routes, and classical ornament and sculpture crown the plan.

Design principles

  • Start from the plan: a main axis, one or two cross axes, and rooms in a hierarchy of size from the entrance hall to the grand salon.
  • Compose the front as a symmetric tripartite order: a base, a colonnade or giant order, and an attic or mansard roof.
  • Mark the central pavilion and the end pavilions by projecting 0.5 to 2m and by a higher roof.
  • Use paired columns and a rich classical entablature on the main storey, with sculpture groups on the attic.
  • Make a grand stair or hall of 15 to 25m height as the climax of the plan, in marble and gilding.
  • Join the building to the city: a front on a square or an avenue, with the axis continued by the street.

Anti-patterns

  • Plans without an axis or a clear hierarchy of rooms
  • Bare undecorated walls and flat roofs without sculpture
  • Asymmetric, picturesque compositions
  • Exposed steel frames and visible industrial structure on the front
  • Gothic or Romanesque motifs in the main order
  • Small, modest rooms in the main sequence

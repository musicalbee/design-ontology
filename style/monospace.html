Monospace

typeface · 1874–present · also: Monospaced, Fixed-pitch, Typewriter type, Code font

Every character takes the same width, as on a typewriter or a terminal. The narrow letters get serifs or wide arms and the wide letters are squeezed. It is the voice of code, data, forms and the typewritten document.

Core thesis

When every character has the same width, columns align without a grid and each character is a unit the reader can count.

Design principles

  • Give every glyph the same advance width, 0.50–0.62 em; 0.60 em is the common value.
  • Widen the narrow letters (i, l, j, r, f, t) with long serifs or arms, so they fill their cell.
  • Squeeze the wide letters (m, w, M, W) and lighten their joins, so they do not look darker than the rest.
  • Make look-alike characters different: 0 with a slash or a dot, l with a tail, 1 with a flag and a foot, and a clear I.
  • Hold a large x-height, 0.70–0.80 of the cap height, for small sizes on screens.
  • Keep the stroke contrast low, 1.0–1.4:1.

Anti-patterns

  • Proportional figures or kerning in a code font
  • An O and a 0 that look the same
  • Ligatures that hide the real characters from a reader who copies the code
  • Monospace for long reading prose
  • Justified text

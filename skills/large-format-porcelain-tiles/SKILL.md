# Large-format porcelain tiles

> One large, dense, thin sheet of ceramic makes a seamless surface that holds the look of another material.

Very hard, dense tiles that a factory presses in sizes of 60 by 60 cm and above, up to slabs of 3 m. A digital printer gives the surface the look of stone, wood or concrete. Fine joints make the wall or floor read as one skin.

**Period:** 1980–present · **Era:** Plural and contemporary · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Porcelain stoneware slabs, Large porcelain slabs, Gres porcellanato, Sintered stone slabs

## When to load this skill

Load it when the user asks for: "large format tile", "porcelain slab", "porcelain stoneware", "stone look tile", "rectified tile", "seamless tile floor", "gres porcellanato".

Good for: a modern floor or wall with a stone or concrete look, a worktop or facade in thin ceramic, a minimal interior with few joints.

Do not use it when:
- the brief needs a hand-made or irregular tile
- the brief needs a glossy glazed colour
- the brief needs a historic look

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Large-format porcelain tiles, you MUST:

1. Press the tile to 60 by 60 cm or more, and up to 120 by 280 cm or 160 by 320 cm for a slab.
2. Fire the body to less than 0.5 per cent water absorption, so that it takes frost, stain and wear.
3. Keep the tile thin, 6 to 12 mm, for a wall, and 20 mm for an outdoor floor.
4. Print the surface by inkjet with a design from stone, wood, concrete or metal, and vary the image over many slabs.
5. Rectify the edges and set the joints to 1 to 2 mm, so that the surface reads as one sheet.
6. Choose a matt or satin surface of 5 to 30 GU, and add a texture that follows the print.
7. Use the slab for floors, walls, worktops and facades in one material, with no change at the corner.
8. Plan the cut and the layout in advance, because a slab has a high cost of break.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** A thick, soft body that absorbs water.
- **NEVER:** A heavy ornamental border.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Large square and rectangular floor and wall tiles, thin slabs, worktop slabs, skirting and step pieces.
- **Profile:** A flat, rectified face with sharp or slightly softened arrises; a printed texture follows the image.
- **Foot and rim:** The back has a fine grid for adhesive; the edge is cut true and may carry a small chamfer.
- **Proportion:** Common sizes are 60 by 60 cm, 60 by 120 cm and 120 by 240 cm; the thickness is 6 to 20 mm.
- **Rules:**
  - Cut the edge true to within 0.5 mm.
  - Print each slab with a different part of the source image.
  - Match the grout colour to the tile and keep the joint under 2 mm.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** porcelain-stoneware
- **Secondary:** porcelain
- **Avoid:** earthenware · terracotta · creamware
- **Rule:** Use a porcelain stoneware of fine feldspathic clay, pressed at high pressure and fired dense. Keep porous earthenware out.

## Forming

- **Methods:**
  - spray-drying the powder
  - pressing the powder in a large hydraulic press or by continuous compaction
  - drying the slab
  - printing the surface by digital inkjet
  - grinding and rectifying the edge after firing
- **Joins:** Slabs are bonded to the base with a thin adhesive, and the joints are 1 to 2 mm.
- **Details:** A rectified edge, a print that repeats only over many slabs, a fine surface texture and a back with a grid.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** satin-glaze · matte-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** digital-inkjet · screen-print · undecorated
- **Pattern:** A digital image of stone, wood or concrete, with veins that run across slabs, under a thin matt or satin layer.
- **Gloss:** Matt or satin, 5 to 30 GU; a polished face can reach 70 GU.
- **Wear:** The body is very hard and does not stain or freeze; a polished face scratches and the print sits in the top layer.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Neutral stone, concrete and wood tones; colour comes from the image and not from the glaze.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A continuous roller kiln of 100 m or more, fired by gas.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** spray-drying the body · pressing · drying · digital printing · firing in a roller kiln for 40 to 60 minutes · rectifying and polishing
- **Scale:** A line produces several thousand square metres in a day, and a factory prints each slab by inkjet.
- **Signs:** A uniform body, a rectified edge, a print with no repeat in a short run and a fine grid on the back.

### Digital ceramic printing (technology)

An inkjet printer puts ceramic ink onto a tile or a slab, with no contact. The print can show stone, wood or concrete and can change from tile to tile. Origin: ceramics, Castellón, Spain, and Sassuolo, Italy, c. 2000.

- Make the image at 360 dpi or more.
- Print with ceramic inks of metal oxides before the glost firing.
- Vary the image over many tiles, so that no repeat shows.
- Fire the tile so that the ink fuses to the glaze.

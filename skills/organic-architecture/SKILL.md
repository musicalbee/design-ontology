# Organic Architecture

> A building should grow from its site and its use as a living form: the whole and the parts belong together, and the plan flows with the land.

A building grows from its site like a plant. Wright, Aalto and Goff set the plan, the structure and the material in one flowing whole that opens to the land, with no fixed façade or box.

**Period:** 1905–present · **Era:** Fin de Siècle · **Domain:** Architecture · **Also called:** Organic design, Wrightian organic, Usonian organic

## When to load this skill

Load it when the user asks for: "organic architecture", "organic design", "frank lloyd wright", "fallingwater", "usonian", "bruce goff", "grows from its site", "cantilevered terrace", "architecture in harmony with nature".

Good for: houses in a landscape, museums and chapels, hotel and resort concepts, landscape architecture studies.

Do not use it when:
- The site is flat and urban and the brief asks for a tall repeating block.
- The brief asks for a strict modular grid.

## System directives

When designing, describing or rendering buildings and interiors in Organic Architecture, you MUST:

1. Fit the building to the site: set the floor levels to the slope and let rocks, trees and water keep their place.
2. Plan as a continuous flow with few doors, and open each room to 2 or more sides of the landscape.
3. Use the cantilever, the curve and the spiral, in spans of 3 to 6m, to make the structure and the form one thing.
4. Use few materials, 3 or 4, left in their natural state, and run them from the inside to the outside.
5. Compress the entry to 2.0 to 2.3m, then release it into a space of 3.5 to 5m.
6. Design the built-in furniture, the light and the garden as parts of the same form.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** A closed box set on a levelled site.
- **NEVER:** Applied historical ornament.
- **NEVER:** A rigid orthogonal grid that ignores the site.
- **NEVER:** Glossy synthetic surfaces and painted-over materials.
- **NEVER:** Rooms cut off by corridors and doors.
- **NEVER:** A building that dominates the landscape.

## Massing & form

- **Form:** Interlocking horizontal and curved volumes that step down the site and reach out in terraces and cantilevers.
- **Proportion:** Low and wide: 3 to 6 times longer than high, with the main living room open to the view.
- **Roof:** Flat or gently sloping roof planes with deep cantilevers of 2 to 5m, or a roof that follows the hill.
- **Relation to ground:** Anchored to the ground: a rock outcrop, a stone chimney or a terrace ties the house to the land.

## Structure

- **System:** Masonry, reinforced concrete and timber; cantilevered slabs and stone piers tie the house to the site.
- **Span and module:** Cantilevers of 3 to 6m; a planning grid of 1.0 to 1.2m; slab depth of 0.15 to 0.25m.
- **Expression:** The cantilever, the stone pier and the curve of the plan state the structure and the form together.

## Materials

- **Primary:** local stone · reinforced concrete · timber · brick
- **Secondary:** copper · glass · cork · leather
- **Finish:** Natural and unpainted: rough stone, board-formed concrete and stained timber, left to age.

## Facade

- **Composition:** A layered front of terraces, parapets and glass: horizontal slabs and stone piers, with no single flat wall.
- **Openings:** Corner windows, glass walls and clerestories; the window-to-wall ratio is 30 to 60%.
- **Solid to void:** Balanced and open: 40–65% solid.

## Light & space

- **Daylight:** Light enters from several sides, low under the eaves and high through clerestories, and the garden extends into the room.
- **Spatial sequence:** A compressed entry gives way to a flowing living space, with a hearth or a water view as the end of the sequence.
- **Interior character:** A free plan with a ceiling of 2.4 to 3.6m, built-in seats and shelves, a stone hearth and natural materials.

## Ornament

- **Attitude:** Ornament is structural and natural: the grain, the stone course and the patterned block carry it, and no applied motif.
- **Details:** patterned textile block · built-in furniture · stone hearth · planters and terraces · spiral ramps · natural timber grain

### Cantilever (process)

A beam or slab fixed at one end that carries load beyond its support, so that it seems to hang in the air. Origin: architecture, Northern Europe, c. 1300.

- Fix the beam at one end and anchor it with a back span of at least 1 part to every 2 parts of the overhang.
- Keep the free end thin; a depth of 1/8 to 1/10 of the span suits steel and concrete.
- Repeat the horizontal line in a run of 2 or 3 projecting planes to give a strong shadow.

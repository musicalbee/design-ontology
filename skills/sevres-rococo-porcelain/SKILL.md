# Sèvres rococo porcelain

> A soft, glassy body takes a deep, even ground colour that no hard paste can match; the painter then leaves white windows for the flowers.

Soft-paste porcelain of the royal factory at Sèvres. A glossy ground colour, such as bleu céleste or rose, frames white reserves that carry flowers and birds, with fine gilt edges. Louis XV and Madame de Pompadour were its patrons.

**Period:** 1756–1775 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Sèvres soft-paste porcelain, Vincennes and Sèvres rococo, Pompadour porcelain

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Sèvres rococo porcelain, you MUST:

1. Fire a glassy soft paste with a lead-rich glaze at about 1100 °C.
2. Lay a ground colour, such as a sky blue or a rose, in an even layer over the glaze.
3. Leave white reserves in the shape of a cartouche, and paint flowers, birds or scenes in them.
4. Outline each reserve with a fine band of gold, and add small gold dots and scrolls.
5. Model the form in curves: a double curve at the rim, a scroll handle, a shell foot.
6. Mark the base with two crossed L letters and a date letter, so that each piece can be dated.
7. Leave the figures in unglazed biscuit, with a soft, matt surface like marble.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Plates with a scalloped rim, tureens known as pots à oille, sauceboats with shell handles, and large cooler bowls.
- **Profile:** A soft double curve at the rim and a swelling body; the edge is scalloped or has a wavy moulded border.
- **Foot and rim:** A low, narrow foot ring with a glazed base; the rim is wavy, with a gilt line on the edge.
- **Proportion:** Plate diameter 23 to 25 cm and wall 2 to 3 mm thick; the ground colour covers about one half of the surface.
- **Rules:**
  - Mould the border in a wavy outline with 6 or 8 scallops.
  - Make the reserve one third of the plate width, in the shape of a cartouche.
  - Gild the edge, the handle and the line of each reserve.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** soft-paste-porcelain
- **Secondary:** ormolu · silver
- **Avoid:** european-hard-paste · earthenware · stoneware
- **Rule:** Use a soft paste of glass frit and white clay, which fires at a lower heat than a hard paste and takes a rich ground colour. Do not use hard paste: it came to Sèvres only after 1770.

## Forming

- **Methods:** press moulding in plaster moulds · slip casting of figures and vases · modelling of the master model · painting a ground colour over the glaze · painting flowers in the reserves · gilding with honey gold
- **Joins:** Cast handles and flowers are luted with slip; gilt bronze mounts are fixed to large vases after the firing.
- **Details:** Fine gilt lines, a date letter in a double L mark, and a painter mark in a small sign.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** coloured-gloss · biscuit
- **Decorations:** overglaze-enamel · gilding · relief-moulding · pierced
- **Pattern:** Flower sprays, birds, putti and garden scenes in white reserves, on grounds of sky blue, rose, apple green or yellow, with gold scroll work.
- **Gloss:** Deep, liquid gloss, about 85 to 95 GU on the glazed parts; the biscuit is matt.
- **Wear:** The gold wears first; the soft glaze can scratch, and the ground colour can craze in a fine net.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A deep, flat ground colour in one tone, white reserves, soft flower colours, and gold lines.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired kiln that fires a biscuit, then a glaze; separate muffle kilns for enamel and gold.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 1100 °C · glaze firing · firing of the ground colour · painting and firing of the reserves · gilding and a last gold firing
- **Scale:** A royal factory of about 200 workers, with painters, gilders, sculptors and chemists.
- **Signs:** Crossed L marks with a date letter, a painter mark, and an even, glassy ground colour.

### Gold pink enamel (material)

A soft rose-pink overglaze enamel that colloidal gold makes. Painters mix it with white enamel to make a range of tints from deep rose to white. Origin: ceramics, Jingdezhen, China, c. 1720.

- Dissolve gold in acid and precipitate it with tin to make a purple powder.
- Mix the powder with a lead glass frit and grind it to a fine paste.
- Mix the pink with white enamel in different ratios to make tints.
- Paint the tints on the fired glaze and fire again at 760 to 800 °C.

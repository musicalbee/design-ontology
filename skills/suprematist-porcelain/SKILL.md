# Suprematist porcelain

> A pot is a Suprematist object when its form is built of cylinders, cubes and slabs, and the paint on it is a flat plane of colour.

Tea and coffee ware in white porcelain from the State Porcelain Factory in Petrograd. Malevich and his pupils turned the plain shapes of Suprematist painting into pots, cups and jugs in blocks, planes and bars.

**Period:** 1923–1935 · **Era:** Modern ceramics · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Suprematist ceramics, Malevich teapot, Soviet avant-garde porcelain

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Suprematist porcelain, you MUST:

1. Build the form from simple solids: a cylinder, a block, a slab and a cone.
2. Keep the surface white, so that the shape shows first.
3. Paint flat planes in red, black and one other colour, with sharp edges.
4. Place the planes at an angle to the form, as in the painting.
5. Make the spout, the handle and the lid a part of the structure, not a decoration.
6. Cast the form in plaster moulds in a repeatable series.
7. Use no flowers, no gilding and no relief.
8. Fire the porcelain hard to about 1400 °C for a dense white wall.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** A tea and coffee service built from solids: a pot as a stack of blocks, a cup as a cylinder with a slab handle, a jug as a tilted cube. The forms are plain, hard and architectural.
- **Profile:** The pot has a flat top and straight sides, and the spout is a sloping slab or a tube. The cup stands straight, with a square-section handle.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat foot with a sharp edge; the rim is cut flat, and the lid sits flush as a plane.
- **Proportion:** A teapot is 15 to 20 cm high and a cup is 6 to 8 cm high; the wall is 2 to 3 mm thick and the edges are sharp.
- **Rules:** Build the form from three or four solids. · Cut the edges sharp and keep the faces flat. · Fit each part into the structure of the whole. · Limit the colour to flat planes of two or three tones.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** european-hard-paste
- **Secondary:** porcelain
- **Avoid:** earthenware · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a hard, white hard-paste porcelain that takes a sharp mould edge. Keep a soft or coloured body out: the white is the ground of the colour.

## Forming

- **Methods:** making a master model in plaster · slip casting in plaster moulds · fettling the seams and sharp edges by hand · spraying or painting flat overglaze colour · firing in saggars in a bottle kiln
- **Joins:** Handles and spouts are cast apart and fixed with slip while the casting is damp; the seam is cut away to a sharp line.
- **Details:** A sharp edge on every face, a hard cut seam and a painted trademark under the base, with the year of the design.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** clear-feldspathic · biscuit
- **Decorations:** overglaze-enamel · decal · hand-banding · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Flat planes in black, red and white, or a single black square or bar on a white form; the lines are sharp and set at angles.
- **Gloss:** High gloss on the glazed face, about 85 to 100 GU; some parts are left as dull biscuit.
- **Wear:** Enamel wears at the edges and the rim. The glaze keeps its gloss and the white stays clean.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** White porcelain with black and red flat planes, and a little blue or yellow. The painting uses two or three tones.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal- or wood-fired bottle kiln for the biscuit and the glaze, and a muffle kiln for the enamel.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 950 °C · dipping in clear glaze · glaze firing at about 1400 °C · painting in flat overglaze enamel · enamel firing at about 800 °C
- **Scale:** A State factory made small series of the forms in Petrograd and Leningrad, with a small studio of artists.
- **Signs:** A sharp edge, a flat plane of colour and a mark of the State factory under the base.

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Overglaze enamelling (process)

Coloured glass enamels are painted on a glazed and fired piece. A third firing at a low heat fixes them on the glaze. Origin: ceramics, Jingdezhen, Jiangxi, China, c. 1465.

- Fire the piece with a clear glaze.
- Paint the design with enamels of red, green, yellow and blue.
- Fire it again in a small muffle kiln at 750 to 850 °C.
- Add gold in a last low firing, when the design needs it.

### Primary shapes (motif)

The circle, the square and the triangle in flat colour, used as the whole picture or as a sign. Origin: painting, Petrograd, Russia, 1915.

- Use 1 to 3 shapes at large size; let one shape fill 30% to 60% of the frame.
- Fill each shape with one flat colour; no gradient and no outline.
- Put the shapes on a plain ground with 30% or more empty space.

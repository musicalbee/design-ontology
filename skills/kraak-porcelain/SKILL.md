# Kraak porcelain

> One cobalt blue on a white porcelain body, ordered in panels that a quick painter can repeat, makes a pattern that a whole continent wants to buy.

Thin blue-and-white export porcelain from Jingdezhen. Plates and bowls have a rim in 8 panels that frame plants and sacred objects, and a central scene. Merchants of Portugal and the Netherlands sold it across Europe.

**Period:** 1570–1644 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Kraakware, Wanli export porcelain, Chinese export blue and white

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Kraak porcelain, you MUST:

1. Divide the rim into 8 panels, in turn broad and narrow, and fill each with a plant, a jewel or a precious object.
2. Paint a single scene in the well: a bird on a rock, a deer, or a vase of flowers.
3. Paint the cobalt under the glaze with a loaded brush, and shade the blue with a pale wash.
4. Throw the porcelain very thin, with a wall 2 to 3 mm thick, so that it is light and translucent.
5. Use a clear glaze with a slight blue-grey tone and fire the dish in a wood-fired kiln.
6. Leave the edge of the rim with a thin brown iron line, where the glaze runs thin.
7. Pack the plates in stacks of 20 or more for the ship; a set is of one size and one pattern.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Wide dishes with a flat rim, rimmed bowls with a flared lip, small plates, and ewers and jars for export.
- **Profile:** A shallow well and a wide rim that is flat or slightly lobed; some bowls have a broad rim that folds out like a cap.
- **Foot and rim:** A narrow foot ring with a sandy, unglazed base and a brown rim line; the foot of a dish is often chipped, from the kiln sand.
- **Proportion:** Dish diameter 20 to 50 cm and wall 2 to 4 mm thick; the rim is about one third of the radius.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the rim flat and one third of the radius, so that the panels have room.
  - Fire the dishes on a bed of sand, and leave the sand marks on the foot.
  - Make the wall thin and light; the weight must be low for the ship.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** porcelain
- **Avoid:** earthenware · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a white kaolin and petuntse porcelain that fires dense and translucent. Do not use earthenware: the whole value is the thin white body.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the wheel · turning the foot and the well when leather-hard · painting the cobalt on the raw body · stacking the dishes on sand in saggars
- **Joins:** Spouts and handles of ewers are luted with slip; a lid fits on a rebated seat.
- **Details:** Light sand marks on the foot, a thin brown line at the rim, and a small blue blur where the cobalt sank into the glaze.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** clear-feldspathic
- **Decorations:** underglaze-cobalt · incised
- **Pattern:** Panels of Buddhist emblems, flowers and jewels on the rim; a deer, a bird, or a flower vase in the well.
- **Gloss:** Soft gloss, about 60 to 80 GU, with a faint blue-grey glaze.
- **Wear:** The glaze stays hard; the brown rim line and the sandy foot show the ship and the market.

## Palette

### Cobalt underglaze painting (process)

Cobalt oxide is painted on the raw or bisque body. A clear glaze covers it, and one firing turns the paint a deep blue. Origin: ceramics, Jingdezhen, Jiangxi, China, c. 1330.

- Grind cobalt ore and mix it with water.
- Paint the design on the dry body with a brush.
- Dip the piece in a clear glaze.
- Fire it once at 1250 to 1300 °C.

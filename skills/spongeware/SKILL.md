# Spongeware

> A sponge turns a cheap white pot into a coloured one in a few seconds, so that a worker without training can decorate a hundred pieces in an hour.

Cheap white earthenware that potters dabbed with a cut sea sponge dipped in colour. The soft blue or green mottle fills a bowl, a jug or a canister with little skill.

**Period:** 1840–1930 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Spatterware, Sponge-decorated earthenware, Sponged export ware

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Spongeware, you MUST:

1. Make a plain, white-bodied pot in a mould or on a jigger, with a wall 4 to 6 mm thick.
2. Dab the colour on the biscuit with a cut sponge dipped in a stain; use 1 to 4 colours.
3. Stamp the sponge in a pattern of dots, or dab it in a loose mottle.
4. Keep a plain white band at the rim and the foot for contrast.
5. Use blue first, then green, brown and red; add a hand-painted line or a flower in some pieces.
6. Dip the pot in clear glaze and fire it at about 1050 to 1100 °C.
7. Make useful shapes: a mixing bowl, a jug, a butter dish and a canister.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Mixing bowls, pitchers, butter dishes, canisters, chamber pots and plates; the forms are plain and stack well.
- **Profile:** A deep bowl with a flared wall and a flat foot; a jug with a round belly and a strap handle.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat foot or a thin turned foot ring left clear of the sponge; a plain rolled rim or a ring of white.
- **Proportion:** A mixing bowl is 18 to 28 cm wide; wall 4 to 6 mm; a jug 15 to 20 cm high.
- **Rules:** Keep a clear band at the rim for the sponge to end on. · Use plain, moulded shapes that a worker can decorate fast. · Glaze the outside and the inside.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** pearlware · stoneware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · terracotta
- **Rule:** Use a white or cream earthenware so that the sponge colour shows. American work also uses a pale stoneware. Keep red clay out: it dulls the colour.

## Forming

- **Methods:** jiggering and press moulding · sponging the biscuit with a cut sponge · hand-painting a line at the rim · dipping in clear glaze
- **Joins:** Handles are cast or pressed and fixed with slip; moulded ribs run on the outside of some bowls.
- **Details:** The soft edge of a sponge print, small gaps between the dabs, and a clear white band at the rim.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** sponge-print · hand-banding · underglaze-polychrome · undecorated
- **Pattern:** A loose mottle of blue, green or brown dabs in a ring round the body; some pieces add a painted flower, a bird or a band.
- **Gloss:** A clear gloss of about 60 to 85 GU; the sponge colour sits under it.
- **Wear:** The glaze crazes with age in a fine net; the colour stays under it.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Blue, green and brown dabs on white; a red or yellow accent in the more costly pieces.

### Sponge stamping (process)

A piece of sea sponge is dipped in ceramic colour and dabbed or stamped on a biscuit pot. The soft print is quick and needs little skill. Origin: ceramics, Scotland and Staffordshire, c. 1840.

- Cut a sponge into a small shape, about 2 to 4 cm wide.
- Dip the sponge in a stain of one colour.
- Dab the sponge on the biscuit in rings or in a loose mottle.
- Leave a band of 1 to 2 cm at the rim clear, then dip the pot in a clear glaze.

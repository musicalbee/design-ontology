# Rococo court dress

> A court gown shows rank by the width of the skirt, the weight of the silk and the hours of hand work in the trim.

The court dress of Louis XV: a wide-hipped silk gown with Watteau back pleats, lace ruffles and flower brocade, copied at the courts of Europe.

**Period:** 1720–1789 · **Era:** Court and guild · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Robe à la française, Louis XV court dress, Watteau-back gown

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Rococo court dress, you MUST:

1. Hold the skirt out on side hoops 1 m across for day and up to 3 m across for court.
2. Boned stays shape the torso into a stiff cone; lace the stays tight so that the back stays straight.
3. Cut the back as two box pleats that fall free from the neck to the floor (the Watteau pleat).
4. Open the front of the skirt over a petticoat in the same silk or a contrast silk.
5. Fill the V of the bodice with a stomacher, and cover it with bows or embroidery.
6. End the sleeve at the elbow in two or three tiers of lace ruffles.
7. Choose small flower sprays on a pale ground; keep the brocade pattern in scale with the skirt.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A wide oval skirt on side hoops, over a tight, stiff, conical bodice with a point at the front.
- **Line:** A low square neckline, a V-shaped stomacher and loose pleats that fall from the neck to the floor at the back.
- **Fit:** Tight and boned at the torso, free at the back where the box pleats hang, and very wide at the hip.
- **Proportion:** The waist is 55 to 65 cm. Day hoops are about 1 m across. Court hoops are up to 3 m across. The bodice ends in a point below the natural waist.
- **Rules:**
  - Lace the stays from the back, and pull the bust up into a flat square neckline.
  - Tie the hoops at the waist so that the skirt is wide at the side and flat at the front and back.
  - Open the skirt at the front in an inverted V to show the petticoat.
  - Trim the edge of the skirt, the sleeve and the neckline with the same ruffle, bow or ruche.
  - Let the hem touch the floor for court and clear the ankle for day.

## Fabrics & materials

- **Primary:** brocade · satin · taffeta · damask · velvet
- **Secondary:** bobbin-lace · needle-lace · silk-twill · glazed-chintz
- **Avoid:** denim · jersey · spandex-jersey · polar-fleece
- **Rule:** Choose a silk with body and a bright surface: brocade and satin for the gown, taffeta for the petticoat, velvet for the coat.

## Construction

- **Methods:** boned stays under the bodice · side hoops of cane and whalebone · box pleats stitched at the back neck · gown pinned to a stomacher · lace sleeve ruffles sewn in tiers · trim ruched and gathered by hand
- **Closures:** Pins at the front, hooks and laces at the back of the stays; the stomacher is pinned to the stays.
- **Details:** Engageantes (lace cuffs) at the elbow, a ruched robings trim down the front, a choker ribbon and a small powdered coiffure to finish.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** embroidered · glazed · block-printed · matte
- **Pattern:** Small flower sprays and ribbons woven into the silk on a pale ground; painted or printed chintz for the informal gown.
- **Gloss:** Satin and taffeta at 40–70 GU; brocade shows a mix of matte ground and bright float.
- **Wear:** Silk splits at the fold and the pleat; a gown is re-trimmed and re-cut for a new owner, so old pieces show many alterations.

## Making

- **Processes:** silk weaving on a Lyon draw loom · dressmaking by a marchande de modes and a couturière · pleating and draping on the body · embroidery and lace work by hand
- **Scale:** Made to order, one gown at a time, by a dressmaker and a trimmer; a court gown took many weeks.
- **Signs:** Hand-sewn pleats, pinned fronts, silk ruched by hand, and a lining that is cut separate from the gown.

### Figured silk weaving (process)

The weaving of patterns into silk cloth on a draw loom, so that flowers and ribbons appear in the weave of the cloth. Origin: fashion, Lyon, France, c. 1536.

- Set up the loom with a pattern of cords that lift the warp threads in a set order.
- Weave the ground in satin or taffeta, and float the coloured or gold weft to make the pattern.
- Press the cloth and cut it with the pattern in scale with the gown.

# Biscuit porcelain figures

> When the glaze and the colour are gone, only the form is left, so that a porcelain figure can look like fine marble in the hand.

Unglazed white porcelain figures that look like marble and show the form with no colour. Vincennes and Sèvres made them after the paintings of Boucher and the models of Falconet, for the dessert table and the shelf.

**Period:** 1751–1800 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Sèvres biscuit, Vincennes biscuit, Unglazed porcelain figures

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Biscuit porcelain figures, you MUST:

1. Leave the figure without glaze after the first firing, so that the surface stays matt and white.
2. Fire a soft-paste body to about 1100 °C, so that the grain is fine and the edge is soft.
3. Model the figure after a painter or a sculptor: a pastoral group, a child, a classical goddess.
4. Cast the figure from piece moulds, and finish each seam with a knife and a damp brush.
5. Keep the surface smooth, with a fine grain like sugar, and a slight warm cream tone.
6. Make the figure 15 to 40 cm high, on a plain base with a small moulded edge.
7. Use no enamel. Allow a single touch of gilt or a coloured glaze on the base only.
8. Show the folds, the hair and the ribbons in sharp, shallow relief that catches light.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Pastoral and mythological figures: children, nymphs, bathers, shepherd groups and small busts, in a calm classical pose.
- **Profile:** A smooth, rounded outline with soft flesh and clear folds; the pose is natural and turns only a little.
- **Foot and rim:** A plain round or oval base with a small moulded edge, often on a rock or a tree trunk that gives support to the figure.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 40 cm; the wall is 3 to 5 mm thick, and the proportion is natural, after a child or a young adult.
- **Rules:**
  - Leave all of the surface without glaze.
  - Keep the grain fine and the surface smooth.
  - Use a rock, a tree stump or a column as a support.
  - Take the pose from a known painting or a sculpture.
  - Show flesh by smooth, soft planes and cloth by sharp, shallow folds.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** soft-paste-porcelain
- **Secondary:** european-hard-paste
- **Avoid:** earthenware · creamware · stoneware · terracotta
- **Rule:** Use a fine, white, dense paste that fires to a matt, sugar-like surface. After 1769 a hard paste also serves; keep the colour a pale warm white.

## Forming

- **Methods:** modelling of a master figure by a sculptor · piece moulding in plaster moulds · casting of parts in slip · joining and fettling of seams by hand · biscuit firing without a glaze
- **Joins:** The parts are cast apart and joined with slip. The seams are cut and sponged until they disappear, so the surface is one smooth plane.
- **Details:** A fine matt grain, a small vent hole in the base, and a sharp edge to the folds that no glaze rounds.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** biscuit
- **Decorations:** undecorated · relief-moulding · incised
- **Pattern:** No colour. The only pattern is the relief of the form: folds, hair, ribbons, leaves and a few incised lines.
- **Gloss:** Matt, 0 to 5 GU, with a soft sheen like marble.
- **Wear:** The surface takes dust and stains, and it marks easily with oil from the hand. Chips show a white, sugary body.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** One warm white and its shadow tones, so that the light on the form is the only colour.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired kiln with saggars at Vincennes, later a larger kiln at Sèvres.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** casting and fettling · single firing without glaze at about 1100 to 1250 °C · hand polishing of the surface · optional touch of gilt on the base
- **Scale:** A royal manufactory with sculptors, moulders and repairers; hundreds of figures a year after 1757.
- **Signs:** A matt surface with no gloss, a fine vent hole, a sharp edge to the folds and a mark under the base.

### Modular figure assembly (process)

A figure is made in many standard parts, such as heads, torsos, arms and props, and the parts are joined and finished by hand. One set of moulds gives many different figures. Origin: ceramics, Lintong, Shaanxi, China, c. 221 BCE.

- Model the figure and cut it into parts.
- Press or cast each part in its own mould.
- Join the parts with slip while the clay is soft.
- Finish the joins and the face by hand, so that each figure differs.

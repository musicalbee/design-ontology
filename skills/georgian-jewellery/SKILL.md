# Georgian Jewellery

> In candlelight a jewel must return the flame. A sheet of foil under the stone and a closed setting make a small gem glow like a large one.

Jewels of the Hanoverian reigns: foil-backed gems and paste in closed silver and gold settings, bright cut steel, pierced bows and flower sprays. They were made by hand to shine in candlelight.

**Period:** 1714–1837 · **Era:** Court and guild · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Georgian period jewels, Hanoverian paste and gem jewellery

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Georgian Jewellery, you MUST:

1. Back each stone with a thin sheet of coloured foil inside a closed box setting, so that the stone returns candlelight.
2. Set the face of a stone in silver and make the back of the piece in gold. Silver keeps a white stone clear, and gold lies on the skin.
3. Cut diamonds and paste in the old mine cut or the rose cut, with a high crown and many small facets.
4. Make a rivière as one line of graduated stones, with the largest stone at the centre and a collet round each stone.
5. Pierce and chase the metal into bows, ribbons and sprays of flowers that follow the curves of the rococo.
6. Rivet small faceted steel studs to a plate for cut steel, a bright jewel that costs far less than a diamond one.
7. Hang each piece on a ribbon, a slide or a short chain so that it sits at the throat, the ear or the bodice.
8. Keep the back of each piece plain or engraved, because a closed setting has no open back.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A small, flat, closed jewel: a bow, a flower spray or a three-drop earring, set close to the dress and made for a candlelit room.
- **Line:** Curved and pierced: ribbons, leaves and swags, with the stones set in a graduated row.
- **Fit:** Close to the body: a rivière sits at the base of the neck, a bow brooch lies flat on the bodice.
- **Proportion:** Rivière stones graduate from about 4 mm at the clasp to about 14 mm at the centre. A bow brooch is 5 to 8 cm wide.
- **Rules:**
  - Wear a rivière close at the base of the neck, with the largest stone at the front.
  - Hang a girandole earring in three drops under a bow or a cluster.
  - Pin a bow brooch at the centre of the bodice or on the stomacher.
  - Tie a pendant cross or a locket on a ribbon of velvet at the throat.

## Construction

- **Methods:**
  - closed collet settings with a coloured foil behind each stone
  - silver front over a gold back
  - hand-pierced and chased bows and sprays
  - riveted cut-steel studs on a plate
  - rivière of graduated collets joined by small links
  - hair and enamel under a crystal for a mourning piece
- **Closures:** A hook and eye or a tongue clasp on a necklace; a pin and a C-clasp on a brooch; a wire hook on an earring.
- **Details:** Ribbon bows, giardinetto flower sprays, a table-cut garnet or a rose-cut diamond at the centre, and a plain engraved back.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** foiled · faceted · engraved · enamelled · pierced · cabochon
- **Pattern:** Flower sprays, bows and ribbons, in rows of equal collets; a mourning piece carries a lock of hair or a monogram.
- **Gloss:** High on the stones and the polished silver; the foil gives a deep, even glow of 70 to 90 GU.
- **Wear:** Silver tarnishes to grey, foil loses its silver and the stone goes dull; a worn piece shows its age and keeps its value.

## Making

- **Processes:** hand cutting of stones on a wheel · hand piercing and chasing of gold and silver · foiling of each stone · riveting of cut-steel studs · casting and finishing of small links
- **Scale:** Small workshops in London, Paris and Birmingham; one maker for a whole piece, and a factory for cut steel after about 1760.
- **Signs:** A closed back, tool marks of the chaser, foil behind each stone and an uneven hand finish on the mount.

### Cut steel work (skill)

Small steel studs with many facets, riveted to a plate. They catch the light like diamonds and cost far less. Origin: fashion, Woodstock, England, c. 1700.

- Forge small steel studs and cut each stud with many facets.
- Polish the facets until they shine like a gem.
- Rivet the studs to a metal plate in the shape of a buckle, a brooch or a necklace.

## Organisations

The groups, studios, companies, schools, channels and backers that carried this style.

- **Soho Manufactory** (company, Soho, Birmingham, 1766–1848), made the style. An early factory of Matthew Boulton. Its workers made buttons, buckles and small metal goods, with cut steel ornaments among them, by a division of labour.

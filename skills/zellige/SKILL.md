# Zellige

> Hand-cut pieces of one-colour glazed clay make a pattern that is exact in its geometry and alive in its surface.

Moroccan tile work in which a potter glazes a square tile in one colour, fires it, and then chisels it by hand into small pieces. A worker sets the pieces face down to make a geometric mosaic.

**Period:** 1300–present · **Era:** Medieval glaze · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Zellij, Moroccan mosaic tile, Fez tile mosaic

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Zellige, you MUST:

1. Form a square tile of 10 cm by 10 cm, 12 to 15 mm thick, from local clay, and glaze it in one colour.
2. Chisel each fired tile with a sharp hammer into the pieces of the pattern, and cut by eye along a line.
3. Set the pieces face down on the drawn pattern, then pour a plaster and cement backing over them.
4. Base the geometry on an 8-point or 12-point star, so that every piece fits a known grid.
5. Limit the colours to white, green, blue, yellow, black and a warm brown, each from one oxide.
6. Keep the joints thin, under 2 mm, and accept a small difference in level between pieces.
7. Lay the mosaic as a dado up to a height of about 1.5 m, and finish the wall above it in carved plaster.
8. Use plain single-colour field tiles near a pattern, so that the eye has a place to rest.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Plain square or hexagonal field tiles, chiselled mosaic panels with star patterns, fountain basins, and floors and dados in courtyards and madrasas.
- **Profile:** A flat face with a slight swell: each piece has a lightly irregular surface and a bevelled, hand-cut edge.
- **Foot and rim:** The tile has an unglazed back with a rough, keyed surface for the plaster. Panels end in a frame of carved plaster or cedar.
- **Proportion:** A field tile is 10 cm square and 12 to 15 mm thick; a mosaic piece is 1 to 8 cm across.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the tile square and thick enough to take the chisel.
  - Cut the pieces after the glaze firing, never before.
  - Show the slight wave of the glaze surface; do not polish the tile flat.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** terracotta
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · porcelain-stoneware
- **Rule:** Use a local red or buff clay that fires soft at about 950 °C and takes a thick glaze. Keep dense bodies out: they are too hard for hand cutting.

## Forming

- **Methods:** digging and weathering the clay · pressing the tile in a wooden frame · drying the tile in the sun · chiselling the fired tile into pieces · setting the pieces face down in a drawn pattern
- **Joins:** A backing of plaster and cement joins the pieces; a thin lime mortar fills the gaps.
- **Details:** Chisel burrs on the back, a bevelled edge on the glazed face, small kiln marks, and a slight variation of colour in each batch.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** tile-mosaic · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Star and polygon patterns in green, blue, yellow, black and white, around a central 8-point star; plain single-colour tiles beside the pattern.
- **Gloss:** Glossy with a soft, uneven shine, about 60 to 85 GU.
- **Wear:** The glaze wears at the edges of the pieces and shows a light buff body; old panels turn slightly cloudy.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood or olive-stone kiln, with the tiles stacked on stilts; one firing for the biscuit and one for the glaze.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** a first biscuit firing of the pressed tile · dipping in tin glaze, one colour for each batch · a second firing for the glaze · chiselling the cold tile
- **Scale:** A workshop in Fez makes a few hundred tiles for each firing, and a worker cuts about 1 square metre of mosaic in several days.
- **Signs:** Three or four small stilt marks on the face or edge, a drip line, and a slight change of colour across one tile.

### Cut tile mosaic (process)

Glazed tiles of one colour are cut into small shapes, and the shapes are set face down in a plaster bed to form a pattern. The bed holds the pieces after it dries. Origin: ceramics, Anatolia and Iran, c. 1200.

- Glaze and fire plain tiles in 5 or 6 colours.
- Cut each tile into pieces with a chisel and a hammer.
- Lay the pieces face down on a drawn pattern, with joints under 1 mm.
- Pour a bed of plaster over the back and let it set.

# Della Robbia relief

> Sculpture in clay becomes lasting and bright when a white tin glaze covers it, so that a church relief shines and resists rain like marble.

Glazed terracotta reliefs that Luca della Robbia and his family made in Florence. White figures stand on a blue ground inside a frame of painted fruit and leaves.

**Period:** 1440–1530 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Robbian glazed terracotta, Invetriata relief, Glazed terracotta of Florence

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Della Robbia relief, you MUST:

1. Model the figures in clay at full size; cut the relief into parts that fit the kiln.
2. Cover the figures with a white tin glaze and the ground with a deep blue.
3. Frame the scene with a wreath of fruit, leaves and flowers in green, yellow and orange.
4. Keep the flesh and the clothes white, and add a little manganese purple to the eyes, the hair and the hem.
5. Use one blue for the sky of a lunette or a roundel; leave the glaze thick and even.
6. Fire the work twice, once bare and once glazed; fit the parts with iron pins and lime mortar.
7. Place the relief high on a church front or over a door, where rain and sun reach it.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Round roundels, arched lunettes, altarpieces and garland frames, with figures in high relief that stand clear of a flat blue ground.
- **Profile:** A frame with a half-round moulding; the figures rise 3 to 8 cm and the head of the Virgin turns in a quiet three-quarter pose.
- **Foot and rim:** The frame ends in a flat border or a bead; the back is open and hollow, with ribs to keep the clay from cracking in the kiln.
- **Proportion:** A roundel is about 1 to 2 m across; the clay wall is 3 to 5 cm thick and the work is cut into 6 to 20 parts.
- **Rules:** Make the figure white on a blue ground. · Surround the scene with a fruit wreath. · Make the clay hollow with ribs on the back. · Cut the relief into parts at the folds of the clothes.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** terracotta
- **Secondary:** earthenware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · stoneware · fired-brick · jasperware
- **Rule:** Use a fine, pale terracotta with little grit, so that the white tin glaze stays clean and the modelled detail stays sharp.

## Forming

- **Methods:** modelling by hand in solid clay · hollowing and ribbing the back · cutting into parts before drying · slow drying for weeks
- **Joins:** The parts are fixed to the wall with iron pins and lime mortar; the joint lines follow the folds of the clothes.
- **Details:** Fine finger marks in the clay under the glaze, small glaze drips in the folds and a slight green tint in the white.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** relief-moulding · underglaze-polychrome · undecorated
- **Pattern:** White Madonna and Child or a saint on a blue ground, with a garland of green leaves, yellow lemons, red apples and pine cones as the frame.
- **Gloss:** Satin to gloss, about 50 to 80 GU; the white tin glaze is thick and a little uneven, so it softens the shapes.
- **Wear:** The glaze keeps its colour outside for centuries; some pieces lose a part of the white and show the red clay at the edge.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** White figures, blue ground and a fruit frame in green, yellow and orange; the palette keeps to a few pure, flat colours.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A small wood-fired kiln of the Florentine workshop, used for the bisque and for the glaze; large works needed a big kiln and many parts.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** modelling in clay · a bisque firing at about 900 °C · dipping in a tin glaze, then painting the colours · a glaze firing at about 1000 °C
- **Scale:** A family workshop with a few makers, which supplied churches and chapels in Tuscany and sold to patrons across Italy.
- **Signs:** A thick white tin glaze that collects in the folds, hand marks under the glaze and a hollow back with ribs.

### Tin glazing (process)

A pot is dipped in a glaze with tin oxide in it. The glaze dries to a white, opaque coat on which a potter paints with oxides before the glaze firing. Origin: ceramics, Basra, Iraq, c. 800.

- Fire the pot once to a biscuit at about 950 °C.
- Dip the biscuit in a bath of glaze with 5 to 10 % tin oxide, so that the coat is about 0.5 mm thick.
- Paint on the dry raw glaze with cobalt, copper, manganese or antimony.
- Fire the glaze at about 1000 °C, so that the colours sink into the glaze.

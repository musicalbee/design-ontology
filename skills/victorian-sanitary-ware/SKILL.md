# Victorian sanitary ware

> A new sanitary fitting must look like fine china, so that a family sees a clean, rich object in a room that was once hidden.

The first ceramic bathroom of the home. Basins, pans and cisterns of earthenware and early vitreous china carry blue, brown or polychrome transfer prints, moulded flowers and gilt lines.

**Period:** 1870–1914 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Victorian lavatory ware, Transfer-printed sanitary ware, Late Victorian bathroom ceramics

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Victorian sanitary ware, you MUST:

1. Cover each visible face with a transfer print in one to three colours, such as blue, brown or green.
2. Mould a raised flower, a shell or a dolphin into the rim, the handle and the pedestal.
3. Glaze the bowl white and thick, 0.5 to 1 mm, so that it is easy to clean.
4. Fit the wash-down pan with a plain curved trap, so that 9 litres of water clear the bowl.
5. Shape the basin with a wide rim, 3 tap holes and a small overflow slot at the back.
6. Mount the cistern high on the wall with a pull chain of brass and a porcelain handle.
7. Add gilt lines on the rim of a fine basin only, as the last firing.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Pedestal and bracket washbasins, wash-down pans with a high flared rim, high-level cisterns, and cased baths.
- **Profile:** A bowl with a deep curve and a rolled rim, resting on a fluted pedestal or a cast iron frame with a scroll.
- **Foot and rim:** A wide, flat rim with a moulded edge, a lipped front on the pan, and a flat base that stands on the floor.
- **Proportion:** Basin 550 to 650 mm wide and 12 mm thick at the wall; pan seat height 380 to 420 mm.
- **Rules:**
  - Print the pattern in a band that follows the rim and the bowl.
  - Mould the relief in the same casting, not applied later.
  - Keep the trap and the pan in one piece, so that there is no joint in the bowl.
  - Choose the pattern of the basin and the pan from the same set.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware · vitreous-china · fireclay
- **Secondary:** brass · ironstone
- **Avoid:** bone-china · plastic · stainless-steel · jasperware
- **Rule:** Use a thick, cream or white body that takes a printed pattern, and after 1880 a dense vitreous body that does not hold water.

## Forming

- **Methods:** slip casting in plaster moulds · press moulding of large parts · hand finishing of relief · turning and sponging of seams · transfer printing and glost firing
- **Joins:** The pedestal and the basin join in a plaster mould before the firing; brass taps and chains are fitted after the last firing.
- **Details:** A maker's name moulded under the rim, a registration lozenge in raised letters, and a small spur mark on the base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · sanitary-glaze · clear-feldspathic
- **Decorations:** transfer-print · relief-moulding · gilding · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Stylised flowers, ferns, birds and Japanese-style fans in a band under the rim, and a large flower or a scene in the bowl of a pan.
- **Gloss:** High gloss, about 80 to 95 GU, with a smooth, even clear glaze over the print.
- **Wear:** Fine crazing lines appear in the glaze, and the gilt wears at the rim; the print under the glaze stays sharp.

## Palette

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal-fired bottle oven with saggars, one firing for the biscuit and one for the glost.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** cast in a plaster mould · dry and fettle · biscuit fire at 1100 to 1250 °C · transfer print on the biscuit · dip and glost fire · gilt and a third firing at 750 °C
- **Scale:** Staffordshire and London potteries with hundreds of workers; one works makes many hundreds of pans in a week.
- **Signs:** A stilt mark inside the foot, a print seam where the paper overlaps, and a registration mark on the underside.

### Slip casting (process)

Liquid clay is poured into a plaster mould. The plaster takes water from the clay and a wall forms on the inside of the mould. Origin: ceramics, Staffordshire, England, c. 1745.

- Make a plaster mould in two or more parts.
- Pour slip into the mould and wait 10 to 20 minutes for a wall of 3 to 5 mm.
- Pour out the rest of the slip.
- Open the mould when the clay is leather-hard and fettle the seam.

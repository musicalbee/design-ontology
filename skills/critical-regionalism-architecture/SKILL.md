# Critical Regionalism

> A building should use modern means to answer a specific place: its climate, its materials and its craft give the form, and universal style and nostalgia are both refused.

Modern form rooted in a place. Utzon, Moneo, Bawa, Piano and Zumthor keep the rigour of modernism but take their light, climate, materials and craft from the site, and they refuse both the universal box and the historical joke.

**Period:** 1981–present · **Era:** Postmodern and After · **Domain:** Architecture · **Also called:** Regionalist modernism, Place-based modernism

Good for: museums and cultural centres, spa and hotel concepts, chapels and civic buildings, houses in strong landscapes.

Do not use it when:
- The brief asks for a global brand building that looks the same everywhere.
- The brief asks for a high-rise on a dense plot.

## System directives

When designing, describing or rendering buildings and interiors in Critical Regionalism, you MUST:

1. Read the site first: set the orientation, the openings and the roof by the sun, the wind and the rain of that place.
2. Use materials from within 100 to 300 km, and let their texture and joints be touched and seen.
3. Build a tectonic order: express the joint, the span and the load in details of 20 to 60mm.
4. Control views: frame the landscape with 2 to 4 deliberate openings, and keep the rest of the wall solid.
5. Borrow from local types, such as the courtyard, the veranda or the thick wall, and recast them in modern construction.
6. Move through the building on a sequence of light, shade and touch, and make each room slow and sensory.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

## Massing & form

- **Form:** A low, grounded volume of stone, concrete or timber that follows the terrain and opens to the site.
- **Proportion:** Horizontal and heavy, 1.5 to 4 times wider than high, with deep shade at the edge.
- **Roof:** A deep overhang of 1.5 to 3m, a turf, tile or metal roof shaped by rain and sun.
- **Relation to ground:** Dug into or set along the slope, with a thick base and a path that joins the building to the land.

## Structure

- **System:** Load-bearing stone or concrete walls with timber or steel roofs, in the methods of the local craft.
- **Span and module:** A planning module of 3.0 to 6.0m; wall thickness of 0.4 to 0.8m; roof spans of 6 to 12m.
- **Expression:** The joint, the course and the span are shown at full scale, and the structure is the main ornament.

## Materials

- **Primary:** local stone · board-formed concrete · timber · lime plaster
- **Secondary:** brick · bronze · slate · glass
- **Finish:** Raw, tactile and local: sawn stone, rough concrete, oiled timber and lime plaster that take weather and touch.

## Facade

- **Composition:** Thick walls with few deep, deliberate openings; a base, a body and a shaded edge, with each part made from a local material.
- **Openings:** Framed windows, slots and deep loggias; the window-to-wall ratio is 15 to 35%.
- **Solid to void:** Mostly solid: 65–85% solid.

## Light & space

- **Daylight:** Light is shaped by the site: reflected, filtered and tinted by thick walls, screens and courts, and the room changes with the hour.
- **Spatial sequence:** A path with a threshold, a court and a sequence of rooms of different light, scale and temperature.
- **Interior character:** A ceiling of 2.6 to 5m, tactile walls, natural material at hand height and small dark places next to bright ones.

## Ornament

- **Attitude:** Ornament comes from craft and joint: the stone course, the timber detail and the shadow line, with no applied symbol.
- **Details:** stone coursing · exposed timber joints · shadow gaps · screens and courts · hand-cut details

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Exposed joinery (process)

A joint that stays in view, so that the way of making is part of the look. Origin: interior, Giza, c. 2500 BCE.

- Cut the joint in the main wood: a through-tenon of 6 to 12mm shows beyond the face.
- Peg the joint with a wooden pin of 8 to 12mm; do not hide it with filler or paint.
- Use one joint type in a room or a building; repeat it at each corner.

### Limewash (material)

A thin wash of slaked lime that dries to a soft, matt white with a faint cloud of brush marks. Origin: architecture, Levant, c. 7200 BCE.

- Mix slaked lime with water to a thin milk, and apply 2 to 3 coats with a wide brush.
- Cross the brush strokes between coats, and let each coat dry for 4 to 24 hours.
- Keep the finish matt; the surface must take light with no sheen.
- Use pale tints only: white, sand and chalk blue.

### Shadow gap (process)

A narrow recessed joint between two surfaces that casts a shadow, so that each surface seems to float apart from the next. Origin: architecture, Verona, c. 1958.

- Cut the gap 10 to 25mm wide and 10 to 20mm deep.
- Hold the gap at a constant width along its full length.
- Use it where new meets old: between a wall and a ceiling, a floor and a skirting, or a new insert and a historic wall.

# English slipware

> A country potter needs no costly colour: red clay, white slip from a quill, and a lead glaze give a bold picture.

Red earthenware that English country potters covered with a clear lead glaze and trailed with cream and brown slip. Large dishes, posset pots and jugs carry bold pictures and rhymes.

**Period:** 1670–1800 · **Era:** The porcelain race · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Staffordshire slipware, Slip-decorated earthenware, Wrotham ware

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in English slipware, you MUST:

1. Throw the body of a red-firing local clay; the wall is 5 to 8 mm thick.
2. Cover the front with a white slip when the clay is leather-hard.
3. Trail liquid slip from a quill pipe in lines 2 to 4 mm wide and in dots.
4. Draw a picture on a large dish: a king, a mermaid, a pelican or a flower.
5. Write a name, a date or a rhyme in capital letters round the rim.
6. Dip the piece in a clear lead glaze; the slip turns cream and the clay turns amber to brown.
7. Fire the glaze at 1000 to 1080 °C in one firing after the biscuit.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Large round dishes up to 45 cm wide, posset pots with two handles, tygs (cups with several handles), jugs and bowls.
- **Profile:** A shallow dish with a wide flat rim and a low well; a jug with a round belly and a pulled strap handle.
- **Foot and rim:** A low foot ring or a flat base; a dish has holes in the rim for a cord; the rim is plain and a little uneven.
- **Proportion:** Dish diameter 30 to 45 cm; wall 6 to 10 mm; a jug stands 15 to 25 cm high.
- **Rules:**
  - Fill the well of a dish with one picture and the rim with a border.
  - Throw jugs on the wheel; press dishes over a hump mould.
  - Leave the back of a dish unglazed or lightly glazed.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** terracotta
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a local red-firing earthenware with iron in it. A white or vitrified body does not suit this ware: the red clay and the cream slip give the colours.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the kick wheel · press moulding over a hump mould · slip trailing from a quill pipe · cutting the foot ring when leather-hard
- **Joins:** Pulled handles are luted with slip at the rim and the belly; a posset pot has a spout fixed in a hole cut in the wall.
- **Details:** Slip lines in raised relief that the finger can feel, drips of glaze, and a name or a date in rough capitals.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** slip-trailing · sgraffito · agateware · incised
- **Pattern:** Kings and queens, birds, mermaids, tulips and the names of the owners, with a border of dots and lines; some dishes carry a rhyme.
- **Gloss:** A warm gloss of about 50 to 75 GU, with a yellow cast over the cream slip.
- **Wear:** The lead glaze wears at the rim and the foot, and the slip lines stay raised.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Warm earth colours: cream slip on red clay under a honey-coloured glaze, with a little brown manganese and copper green.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal- or wood-fired bottle kiln; one firing for the biscuit and one for the glaze.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 950 °C · coating with white slip · trailing the slip · dipping in lead glaze · glaze firing at about 1050 °C
- **Scale:** Small family potteries of 3 to 10 workers in Burslem and Wrotham; the large dishes were made to order for feasts and weddings.
- **Signs:** Raised slip lines, drips of glaze at the foot, and a signature or a date in capitals on the front.

### Slip trailing (process)

Liquid clay, called slip, is trailed from a quill or a pipe onto the leather-hard pot in lines and dots. The lines stay raised under the glaze. Origin: ceramics, Staffordshire, England, c. 1670.

- Mix slip to the thickness of cream.
- Fill a pipe or a bag with the slip.
- Draw lines 2 to 4 mm wide on a pot in a contrast colour.
- Dip the pot in clear lead glaze and fire it at about 1050 °C.

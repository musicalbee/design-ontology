# Albarello apothecary ware

> A drug jar must tell a clerk which drug it holds, so the label is part of the pot: a painted cartouche on a clean white tin glaze.

Tin-glazed earthenware jars and jugs that Italian potters made for the shelf of the pharmacy. The waisted albarello holds dry drugs, and the label names the drug in a painted cartouche.

**Period:** 1450–1750 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Pharmacy jars, Maiolica drug jars, Vasi da farmacia

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Albarello apothecary ware, you MUST:

1. Throw a waisted cylinder with a flared lip and foot, so that a hand holds it and a cord ties on a parchment lid.
2. Make the height 15 to 30 cm and the width a little more than half of the height.
3. Cover the whole pot with an opaque white tin glaze about 0.5 mm thick.
4. Paint a cartouche or a scroll on the front, and write the name of the drug in it in Latin.
5. Paint blue on the raw glaze with cobalt; add manganese purple, copper green and ochre in polychrome work.
6. Paint a foliage ground, the "oak-leaf" or "peacock-feather" pattern, round the label.
7. Give a syrup jar a spout and a strap handle, and a bottle a long neck with a flat mouth.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Albarelli (waisted drug jars), spouted syrup jars, bottles with a long neck and ewers, all made as sets for one pharmacy.
- **Profile:** A cylinder with a concave waist, a shoulder that rises to a short neck and a flared lip that ties on a lid.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat or slightly splayed foot; the rim is a small bead under which a cord ties on the lid of parchment or leather.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 30 cm; the wall is 4 to 8 mm thick; the waist is about 70 % of the width at the shoulder.
- **Rules:**
  - Waist the jar so that fingers hold it and so that jars stand in rows.
  - Keep one label zone on the front, 6 to 10 cm wide.
  - Make a set in one height, so that the shelf looks orderly.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** terracotta
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a buff or pink earthenware that fires soft at about 1000 °C and takes a thick tin glaze. A hard body does not suit it: the glaze must grip a porous clay.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the kick wheel · turning the shoulder and foot when leather-hard · pulling spouts and strap handles · dipping in tin glaze
- **Joins:** Spouts and handles are luted with slip on the leather-hard body; the lip is turned to take a cord.
- **Details:** Thin glaze at the rim, a ring of three stilt marks inside the foot, and a slight blur of blue where the colour sank into the glaze.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** underglaze-cobalt · underglaze-polychrome · lustre · istoriato
- **Pattern:** A cartouche or a ribbon with the name of the drug, set in a ground of oak leaves, foliage, peacock-feather scales or grotesques; some jars show a portrait or a saint.
- **Gloss:** A smooth, deep gloss of about 55 to 80 GU, softer on the inside.
- **Wear:** The tin glaze chips at the lip and the foot and shows the buff body; the painted colour stays fresh.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Cobalt blue on milk-white; polychrome jars add manganese purple, copper green and antimony yellow.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired updraught kiln; one firing for the biscuit and one for the glaze.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 950 °C · dipping in tin glaze · painting on the raw glaze · glaze firing at about 1000 °C · lustre firing in a muffle kiln, for lustred jars only
- **Scale:** Pharmacies ordered sets of 50 to 300 jars; Faenza, Deruta and Venice shipped them to hospitals and convents across Europe.
- **Signs:** A painted cartouche with a Latin drug name, stilt marks in the foot ring, and a bead under the lip.

### Tin glazing (process)

A pot is dipped in a glaze with tin oxide in it. The glaze dries to a white, opaque coat on which a potter paints with oxides before the glaze firing. Origin: ceramics, Basra, Iraq, c. 800.

- Fire the pot once to a biscuit at about 950 °C.
- Dip the biscuit in a bath of glaze with 5 to 10 % tin oxide, so that the coat is about 0.5 mm thick.
- Paint on the dry raw glaze with cobalt, copper, manganese or antimony.
- Fire the glaze at about 1000 °C, so that the colours sink into the glaze.

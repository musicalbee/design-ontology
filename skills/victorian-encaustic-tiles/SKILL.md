# Victorian encaustic tiles

> A pattern that is part of the clay, and not a layer on it, does not wear off under a million feet.

Hard floor tiles with a pattern of clay in a second colour set into the face. Factories pressed them from dust and sold them to churches, halls and houses. The pattern is flat, geometric and mostly red, buff, black and blue.

**Period:** 1840–1910 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Minton encaustic floor tiles, Gothic Revival floor tiles, Victorian geometric floor

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Victorian encaustic tiles, you MUST:

1. Press a recess of 2 to 3 mm in the red body and fill it with a clay of a second colour, so that the pattern goes through the face.
2. Limit the palette to 4 or 5 earth colours: red, buff, black, blue-grey and a cream.
3. Build the floor from tiles of 4 to 6 inches, and join 4 to 16 tiles into one repeat.
4. Draw the pattern from medieval sources: the lily, the quatrefoil, the heraldic beast and the interlace.
5. Surround the main field with a border of narrow tiles, and set a plain tile at the edge.
6. Fire the tile hard with a thin clear lead glaze, or leave the face matt and unglazed on the floor.
7. Add relief and majolica glaze on wall tiles, where nobody walks, to give bright blues and greens.
8. Make each tile the same size to 0.5 mm, so that the joints stay tight and the pattern runs on.

- **NEVER:** A thick, wavy glaze that hides the edges of the inlay.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Square and rectangular floor tiles, border tiles, hexagons, triangles and small wall tiles with relief.
- **Profile:** A flat, hard face with sharp arrises; the inlay is flush with the face, and a tile for the wall has a low raised moulding.
- **Foot and rim:** The back has keys or grooves for mortar; the edge is square and slightly undercut, so that the face joints stay tight.
- **Proportion:** A floor tile is 4 to 6 inches square and 18 to 25 mm thick; a border tile is 2 to 3 inches wide.
- **Rules:**
  - Make every tile square to within 0.5 mm.
  - Keep the lines of the pattern at least 3 mm wide, so that the inlay stays whole.
  - Draw the pattern so that it joins with the next tile on all four sides.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** fired-brick
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · creamware
- **Rule:** Use a dense red earthenware, pressed from nearly dry clay and fired hard. Keep the inlay clays close to the body in shrinkage. Keep fine white bodies out.

## Forming

- **Methods:** pressing the tile from dust or plastic clay in a screw press · pressing a recess with a plaster die · pouring slip of a second colour into the recess · scraping the face flush · drying the tile slowly on a rack
- **Joins:** Tiles are laid in a lime or cement bed, and the repeat joins across the edges.
- **Details:** A sharp edge between two colours, a very slight line where the inlay meets the body, and a maker's mark in the back.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · bare-clay · majolica-glaze
- **Decorations:** encaustic-inlay · relief-moulding · transfer-print
- **Pattern:** A medieval geometric or heraldic repeat in inlaid clay, with a border; wall tiles carry a moulded flower under bright glaze.
- **Gloss:** Matt to satin on the floor, 10 to 40 GU; wall tiles 70 to 90 GU.
- **Wear:** The floor wears evenly because the pattern goes through the face; the glaze lasts a few years under heavy use and the body shows.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Earth reds, buffs and black with one cold blue-grey; wall tiles add jewel colours.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal-fired round bottle kiln or muffle kiln, with the tiles in saggars.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** pressing and filling the recess · drying · biscuit firing in saggars · a second firing with a clear glaze for wall tiles
- **Scale:** Factories made hundreds of thousands of square feet of tile a year by 1870, and sold to the whole British Empire.
- **Signs:** A flush inlay, a sharp edge and a low gloss on the face.

## Techniques

### Dust pressing (process)

Nearly dry clay powder is pressed in a steel die under high pressure to form a tile in one step. The tile needs little drying and keeps its size. Origin: ceramics, Staffordshire, England, 1840.

- Grind the clay and sieve it to a fine powder with about 6 per cent water.
- Fill the steel die and press the powder under a screw or hydraulic press.
- Remove the tile and fire it with no long drying.
- Keep the size of each tile within 0.5 mm.

# Mingei folk craft

> The most beautiful pots are the plain tools of daily life that anonymous craftspeople make in large numbers, with local clay and no wish for fame.

A Japanese movement that began in 1926. Yanagi Sōetsu and the potters Hamada Shōji and Kawai Kanjirō praised plain pots by unknown village potters and made new pots of the same kind.

**Period:** 1926–1975 · **Era:** Modern ceramics · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Mingei pottery, Japanese folk craft movement, Mashiko ware

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Mingei folk craft, you MUST:

1. Use the local clay and the local glaze material of the village, within 10 km of the kiln.
2. Make strong, useful forms: jars, bottles, bowls and large dishes that can stand daily use.
3. Throw quickly and with confidence, and do not correct the small errors of the form.
4. Paint with a wide brush, in 3 to 6 strokes, in iron brown or blue.
5. Pour or dip the glaze in a few motions, and let it overlap and run.
6. Use plant-ash, kaki and nuka glazes, fired at about 1250 °C in a wood-fired climbing kiln.
7. Make the same form many times, and do not sign it as art.
8. Put the pot into daily use: for tea, rice, pickles and flowers.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Large jars, bottles, wide bowls, dishes and flower vessels in a thick, heavy and strong shape.
- **Profile:** A round body with a firm shoulder and a short neck; a jar is wide and low, and a dish has a thick, simple rim.
- **Foot and rim:** A rough, wide foot ring left bare; the rim is thick and has a slight undulation.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 50 cm; the wall is 6 to 12 mm, thick and heavy in the hand.
- **Rules:** Make the form strong and for use. · Leave the throwing marks and the finger marks. · Keep the foot bare and rough.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** stoneware
- **Secondary:** earthenware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · jasperware
- **Rule:** Use a coarse local clay that is dark and iron-rich after the firing. Do not refine the clay to a fine white body.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the kick wheel · coiling of large jars · brush painting with iron and cobalt · pouring and dipping of the glaze · wax resist
- **Joins:** The pieces are thrown whole; the handles are pulled quickly and added by hand.
- **Details:** Visible throwing rings, a rough foot, glaze drips, kiln scars and a loose, rapid brush stroke.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** ash-glaze · tenmoku · shino-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** iron-painting · wax-resist · slip-trailing · incised · undecorated
- **Pattern:** A few strokes of iron brown, lines or leaves, under an ash glaze; a plain kaki brown, with a pour of white glaze over it.
- **Gloss:** Matt to satin, 10 to 50 GU; kaki glaze has a deep gloss.
- **Wear:** Ash glaze is hard; the clay body shows on the rim and the foot, and darkens with use.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A limited range of earth colour: iron brown, rice white, ash green and a little blue.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired climbing kiln of several chambers (noborigama).
- **Atmosphere:** wood-ash
- **Processes:** wedging and kicking of local clay · throwing and brushwork in a single day · dipping or pouring of the glaze · wood firing for 2 to 3 days to about 1250 °C · cooling over 3 days
- **Scale:** A village pottery makes hundreds of pots in each firing; each has a different surface.
- **Signs:** Ash deposits and flashes of colour on the shoulder, spur marks, and a rough bare foot.

## Techniques

### Ash glazing (material)

A glaze that comes from wood or plant ash. The ash melts with the clay and makes a green, yellow or brown glass. Origin: ceramics, China (Shang dynasty), c. 1500 BCE.

- Burn wood or straw and sieve the ash.
- Wash the ash to take out the soluble salts.
- Mix it with clay and feldspar, or use it alone.
- Pour or dip the glaze and fire to 1250 °C or more.

## Media

The salons, schools, magazines, publishers, galleries and platforms that carried this style.

- **Japan Folk Crafts Museum** (gallery, Tokyo, 1936–now), exhibited the style. A museum of everyday craft that Yanagi Soetsu founded to show and teach the beauty of plain, hand-made objects. It made the mingei idea known to makers and designers.

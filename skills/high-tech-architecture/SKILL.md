# High-tech

> A building is a machine of parts: structure, services and circulation are tools to see and to change, and open floors let the use change.

A lightweight steel frame, with the services, the bracing and the lifts shown in colour on the outside. Rogers, Piano and Foster made the machine aesthetic of the 1920s into a flexible, kit-of-parts building.

**Period:** 1970s–1990s · **Era:** Postmodern and After · **Domain:** Architecture · **Also called:** Structural Expressionism, Late Modernism (technological)

Load it when the user asks for: "high-tech architecture", "pompidou", "rogers and piano", "norman foster", "hsbc building", "exposed services", "steel and glass machine", "bolted steel".

Good for: museum and library renders, corporate headquarters, airport and station design, science and lab buildings.

Do not use it when:
- The brief asks for domestic warmth, traditional craft or a heavy masonry building.
- The site has strong rules on a historic roofline or a quiet street front.

## System directives

When designing, describing or rendering buildings and interiors in High-tech, you MUST:

1. Free the floor plate: span 12 to 48m with trusses or tension rods, so that the interior has no columns in the use area.
2. Put services outside, on the facade or the roof: ducts, pipes, lifts and stairs, coloured by function (blue air, green water, yellow power, red escape).
3. Build from a kit of prefabricated parts: steel members, pins, castings and glass panels that are bolted, not welded on site.
4. Show the joints: bolts, pins, cast gerberettes and tension rods are the detail that carries the elevation.
5. Wrap the frame in a thin skin of glass or metal panel, with a window-to-wall ratio of 60 to 100%.
6. Plan for change: a regular bay of 6 to 12m, a raised floor of 150 to 600mm, and walls that move.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** Services hidden in a ceiling void and a cladded riser.
- **NEVER:** Massive concrete or masonry walls.
- **NEVER:** Applied historical ornament or quotation.
- **NEVER:** Welded smooth joints that hide how the part is fixed.
- **NEVER:** Fixed plans with load-bearing partitions.
- **NEVER:** Rough, unfinished, hand-made surfaces.

## Massing & form

- **Form:** A clear rectangular box of 3 to 8 storeys, with the stairs, lifts and ducts in separate bands or towers beside it.
- **Proportion:** Wide and low: 2:1 to 4:1 length to height; the long front is a screen of frame and glass.
- **Roof:** A flat roof with plant, masts and tension-rod trusses; the roof edge is a thin line.
- **Relation to ground:** A raised public plaza or a ground floor open on all sides, so that the building sits like a machine on a platform.

## Structure

- **System:** A steel frame with trusses, tension members and cast steel joints, on a 6 to 12m module.
- **Span and module:** Floor spans of 12 to 48m; the frame bay of 6 to 12.8m; floors are 3.5 to 4.2m storey to storey.
- **Expression:** All members are visible, from the cross-bracing to the pins; the structure is the main decoration.

## Materials

- **Primary:** painted structural steel · glass · stainless steel · aluminium panels
- **Secondary:** tensile fabric · cast steel · perforated metal · raised access floor
- **Finish:** Factory-made and exact: paint in primary colours, brushed stainless, clear glass, and bolted joints.

## Facade

- **Composition:** A frame of columns, bracing and floors in front of glass, with a coloured layer of services and a glazed outside escalator or stair.
- **Openings:** Full-height glazing; the window-to-wall ratio is 60 to 100%, with solid panels only at the cores.
- **Solid to void:** Void leads: 0–40% opaque, mostly as steel members and panels.

## Light & space

- **Daylight:** Light reaches deep plans through full glass fronts, rooflights and atrium; the structure casts a fine line of shadow.
- **Spatial sequence:** A free open floor reached by a long glazed escalator or a lift on the outside; the plan is a field, not a sequence.
- **Interior character:** Bare steel soffits, raised floors, movable partitions and visible services in the ceiling.

## Ornament

- **Attitude:** No applied ornament; the detail of the joint, the colour code of the services and the structure are the image.
- **Details:** cast steel gerberettes · tension rod bracing · colour-coded ducts · glazed external escalators · exposed bolts and pins

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Curtain wall (technology)

A thin outer wall of glass and metal that hangs on the frame of the building and carries no floor load. Origin: architecture, San Francisco, United States, 1918.

- Carry the floors on columns set 1 to 3 m back from the facade line.
- Hang the glass in a grid of mullions at 1.2 to 1.5 m centres.
- Keep the glass part of the facade above 60%.

### Exposed services (process, invented here)

Ducts, pipes, stairs and cables stay in view on the outside or the ceiling, often colour-coded, so the building shows how it works. Origin: architecture, Paris, France, 1977.

- Leave each duct, pipe and cable tray in view; paint them or leave them raw metal.
- Give each system one colour: for example blue for air, green for water, yellow for electricity.
- Keep the floor plate free; put the services on the facade or the ceiling.

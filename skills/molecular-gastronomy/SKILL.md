# Molecular gastronomy

> Cooks can ask why food changes when it is heated, mixed or frozen, and then use the answer to build new textures, temperatures and surprises.

A kitchen that uses the science of food as a tool and a game. Hervé This and Nicholas Kurti named the field in 1988. Ferran Adrià at elBulli and Heston Blumenthal at The Fat Duck made foams, spheres and liquid-nitrogen ice cream.

**Period:** 1988–2011 · **Era:** Modernist and global · **Domain:** Cuisine · **Also called:** Techno-emotional cuisine, Avant-garde cuisine, Science kitchen, Cuisine of deconstruction

- the brief wants a rich classic sauce
- the brief is home or street food

## System directives

When specifying, describing or prompting food and its table: a dish, a menu, a plate, a service, a shop counter or a food photograph in Molecular gastronomy, you MUST:

1. Ask a physical or chemical question about a dish, test it in the kitchen, and keep the answer.
2. Change the form of the ingredient: a liquid becomes a sphere, a purée becomes a foam, a cream becomes a powder.
3. Keep the taste of the ingredient clear while its texture and temperature change.
4. Play with the senses: serve hot and cold in one bite, and add sound, smell or a story to the dish.
5. Cook slowly at an exact low temperature, and measure the heat with a probe.
6. Serve a long sequence of small courses as one show, with each course a new surprise.
7. Work in a research kitchen, record each test, and share the findings.

## Plating & composition

- **Composition:** A small, exact object or a group of objects on a clean surface: a sphere, a foam or a frozen piece, placed with a spoon or in a special vessel.
- **Plate:** A white plate, a glass or a spoon, a slate or a vessel made for one dish: a petri dish, a tube or a bag.
- **Portion:** Twenty to thirty-five courses of 5 to 40 g each; a main piece of 60 g at most.
- **Height:** Low or very small: a sphere of 20 mm, a foam in a glass of 60 mm, a frozen dish that gives off vapour.
- **Rules:**
  - Serve each course in the vessel that fits its form: a spoon for a sphere, a glass for a foam.
  - Place one clear object on a clean surface and leave most of the plate empty.
  - Serve the cold and the hot parts at the last second, so that the temperature contrast stays.
  - Use a surprise of form: a food that looks like another food.
  - Give the order of the bites to the guest, and explain it in one short line.

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Siphon foam (tool, invented here)

A purée or a liquid loaded into a cream siphon with nitrous oxide comes out as a light, warm or cold foam. Origin: cuisine, Roses, Spain, 1994.

- Strain the liquid or purée so that no solid blocks the nozzle.
- Fill the siphon to half and charge it with one cartridge of nitrous oxide.
- Shake the siphon five times and serve at once from the nozzle.
- Keep a hot foam at 60 °C in a water bath.

### Sous vide (process)

Food is sealed in a bag and cooked in a water bath at an exact low temperature, so that the whole piece reaches the same core heat. Origin: cuisine, Roanne, France, 1974.

- Season the food and seal it in a bag with the air removed.
- Set the water bath to the target core temperature, for example 56 °C for beef.
- Cook for the time that the thickness of the piece needs, from 1 to 48 hours.
- Chill or sear the food just before service.

### Spherification (process, invented here)

A liquid dropped into a calcium bath forms a thin skin of alginate gel; the sphere holds its shape on the spoon and bursts in the mouth. Origin: cuisine, Roses, Spain, 2003.

- Mix 0.5 per cent sodium alginate into the flavoured liquid and rest it until the bubbles go.
- Drop the liquid with a spoon into a calcium bath of 1 per cent for 1 to 2 minutes.
- Lift the sphere out and rinse it in clean water.
- Serve within 5 minutes, because the gel keeps setting towards the centre.

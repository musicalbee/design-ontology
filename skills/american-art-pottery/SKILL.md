# American art pottery

> A pottery of artists can make a vase a painting or a leaf: one line paints in slip under glaze, and the other line builds the form in matt colour.

Art pottery from American potteries after 1880. Rookwood painted slip under a clear glaze, and Grueby made thick matt green glazes for vases with leaf forms.

**Period:** 1880–1940 · **Era:** Art pottery and reform · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Rookwood standard glaze ware, Grueby matte ware, American Arts and Crafts pottery

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in American art pottery, you MUST:

1. Paint the design in coloured slip on damp clay, so that the colours blend like a watercolour.
2. Cover the painted piece with a clear glossy glaze, the Standard glaze, and fire it once.
3. Grade the ground from dark brown to orange, olive or yellow with an atomiser spray.
4. Paint one flower, fish or portrait on each vase; the painter signs with a monogram.
5. For a matt line, use a thick glaze of 1 to 2 mm in a single colour, green above all.
6. Mould the leaf and the stem in the wall, and make the glaze pool in the hollows.
7. Mark each piece on the base with a stamp, a date code and a shape number.
8. Fire in oxidation at about 1100 to 1250 °C for earthenware and 1250 °C for stoneware.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Tall vases, bottle vases, lidded jars and planters, with a full belly, a narrow neck and plain, strong lines.
- **Profile:** A swelling curve from a narrow foot to the shoulder; leaf-moulded wall pieces have vertical ribs that rise to the neck.
- **Foot and rim:** A small flat foot with a stamped mark; the rim is a plain or a thick rolled lip.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 50 cm; the wall is 5 to 9 mm thick.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the form simple, so that the painting or the glaze is the surface.
  - Mould the ribs and leaves in the wall for the matt pieces.
  - Stamp the base with the pottery mark and the shape number.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware · stoneware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · jasperware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a local red or buff clay that takes slip and a thick glaze. The body must be sturdy enough to carry a matt glaze of 1 to 2 mm.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the wheel · jiggering and moulding of repeat shapes · slip painting on damp clay · carving and incising in leather-hard clay · spraying with an atomiser
- **Joins:** The foot and the neck are thrown in one piece; handles are pulled and joined by hand.
- **Details:** A stamped pottery mark, a date code, the initials of the decorator, and a glaze that pools in the hollows.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · matte-glaze · coloured-gloss
- **Decorations:** underglaze-polychrome · incised · relief-moulding
- **Pattern:** A flower, a fish, a landscape or a portrait on a dark ground in the Standard line; a plain leaf and a single matt green in the Grueby line.
- **Gloss:** Standard glaze 70 to 90 GU; matt glaze 5 to 20 GU.
- **Wear:** Standard glaze crazes with age; matt glaze keeps a velvet feel and can show finger marks.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A tonal ground from dark brown to orange or yellow, with soft painted flowers; or a single matt green.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal-fired round-down kiln; later a gas kiln.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing of the moulded and thrown body · slip painting and spraying of the ground · dipping or spraying of the clear glaze · one glaze firing at 1100 to 1250 °C
- **Scale:** Some 50 decorators at the largest pottery in the 1890s; thousands of pieces each year, each painted by hand.
- **Signs:** A pottery stamp, a decorator cipher, a date code, and a soft blend of slip colours under the glaze.

### Matt glazing (material)

A thick, dry-looking glaze with a velvet surface. The potter adds alumina or a silica surplus, so that fine crystals make the surface dull. Origin: ceramics, Boston, United States, c. 1898.

- Add alumina or a surplus of silica to the glaze, so that it does not melt to glass.
- Apply the glaze thick, 1 to 2 mm, by dipping or by brush.
- Fire it to the full heat and cool it slowly.
- Wipe the shoulder clean of drips to show the form.

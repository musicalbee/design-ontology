# Digital 3D-printed ceramics

> When a machine builds the form from a file, the vessel can have a shape that no wheel or mould can make, and the layer line becomes the ornament.

Vessels that a machine builds layer by layer from a clay paste, after a digital model. The visible layer lines, the open lattice and the complex curves give the style its look.

**Period:** 2010–now · **Era:** Plural and contemporary · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Additive ceramics, Printed clay vessels, Parametric ceramic vessels

- the brief needs a thick glaze effect
- the brief needs a traditional decoration

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Digital 3D-printed ceramics, you MUST:

1. Draw the form as a digital model with a parametric rule: a twist, a wave, a lattice or a gradient.
2. Print the clay paste in layers of 0.8 to 2 mm with a nozzle of 1 to 4 mm.
3. Keep the layer lines as the surface texture; do not sand them away.
4. Vary the wall in one piece: a solid base, a thin wall and an open lattice at the top.
5. Print porcelain or stoneware paste that holds its shape, and dry it slowly for 3 to 7 days.
6. Fire the piece in one cycle at 1220 to 1300 °C, with a support of kiln setters where it overhangs.
7. Use a thin satin or clear glaze that keeps the ridges, or leave the surface bare.
8. Print a colour pattern with a ceramic inkjet if the piece needs a graphic surface.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** A smooth, wheel-thrown surface with no layer line.
- **NEVER:** A thick, glossy glaze that fills the ridges.
- **NEVER:** A traditional folk or floral decoration.
- **NEVER:** A form that a plain mould could make.
- **NEVER:** A random mess of lines with no rule.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Vases, planters, lidded jars, bottle vases and sculptural vessels with twisted, ribbed, lattice or gradient walls.
- **Profile:** A smooth, complex curve that is built from fine rings: twisted columns, wave walls, open lace and thin double walls.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat printed base that is part of the first layers; the rim is a clean horizontal cut or a lattice edge.
- **Proportion:** Height 10 to 60 cm; wall 1 to 4 mm, and a lattice with openings of 3 to 15 mm.
- **Rules:**
  - Make the form follow a digital rule that the viewer can read.
  - Keep the layer lines at one regular pitch.
  - Design the overhangs to be less than 45 degrees, or add a support.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** porcelain · porcelain-stoneware · paper-clay
- **Secondary:** stoneware
- **Avoid:** bone-china · jasperware · raku-clay
- **Rule:** Use a smooth paste that flows through a nozzle and then holds its shape. Paper clay and fine porcelain are the best at thin walls and overhangs.

## Forming

- **Methods:** 3D printing of clay paste with a screw extruder · parametric modelling of the form · slow drying on a plaster bed · trimming of the base · glaze spraying · inkjet printing of colour
- **Joins:** The vessel is printed in one piece, with no joins; lids and parts are printed separately and fitted by the file.
- **Details:** Regular layer lines, a small seam where each layer starts, precise lattice openings and a flat, exact base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** clear-feldspathic · satin-glaze · matte-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** layer-lines · undecorated · digital-inkjet
- **Pattern:** Fine, regular horizontal ridges that run round the form; a twisted rib; a lattice wall; a colour gradient that is printed in ceramic ink.
- **Gloss:** Matt to satin, 5 to 50 GU; thin glaze keeps the ridges sharp.
- **Wear:** The ridges collect dust and are harder to clean; the thin lattice is brittle, and a thin wall chips at the rim.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A restrained, clean palette that makes the form the main subject: white, pale grey and clay, with an occasional strong colour.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** An electric kiln, with a slow, even oxidation firing.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** digital modelling and slicing of the file · printing of the clay paste in layers · slow drying for 3 to 7 days · biscuit firing at about 900 to 1000 °C · glaze firing at 1220 to 1300 °C
- **Scale:** One piece takes 2 to 20 hours to print; a studio prints one to a few pieces each day.
- **Signs:** Regular layer lines, a faint seam where the layers start, and a clean, exact lattice.

### Clay paste extrusion (technology)

A printer pushes soft clay through a nozzle and builds a form in thin layers. A computer file sets the path of the nozzle. Origin: ceramics, Netherlands and Belgium, c. 2010.

- Draw the form in software and slice it into layers of 1 to 2 mm.
- Load soft clay into the cylinder of the printer.
- Press the clay through a nozzle of 1 to 4 mm and build the wall layer by layer.
- Dry the form slowly, glaze it and fire it.

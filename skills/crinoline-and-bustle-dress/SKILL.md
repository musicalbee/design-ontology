# Crinoline and bustle dress

> The skirt is the dress: a steel cage and then a bustle carry yards of silk, and the couture house sets the shape each season.

The dress of the Second Empire: a vast bell skirt on a steel cage from 1856, then a bustle from 1870, in strong silk.

**Period:** 1856–1889 · **Era:** Victorian dress · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Cage crinoline dress, Victorian bustle dress, Second Empire dress

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Crinoline and bustle dress, you MUST:

1. Carry the skirt on a cage of 12 to 20 steel hoops; the hem is up to 3 m across.
2. Cut the bodice close to the body, with a pointed front and a waist at 56 to 63 cm.
3. Use 10 to 20 m of silk for a day skirt, and up to 40 m for a ball gown.
4. In the 1870s, move the fullness to the back on a bustle pad of wire and horsehair.
5. Drape the overskirt in swags at the back, and add a train for evening.
6. Trim the skirt with flounces, pleats, bows and braid in rows.
7. Use the new strong dyes, so that the colour is deep and bright.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** In the 1860s, a fitted bodice sits over a very wide dome of skirt. In the 1870s and 1880s, the front is flat and the drape forms a shelf at the back.
- **Line:** A low sloped shoulder, a pointed waist, and a skirt that grows into a bell, then moves back into a train.
- **Fit:** Tight and boned at the torso; the skirt stands free of the legs on its cage or its bustle pad.
- **Proportion:** The waist is 56 to 63 cm. The cage hem is up to 3 m across. The bustle shelf is 20 to 30 cm out from the back. The train is 30 to 60 cm long on the floor.
- **Rules:**
  - Lace the corset first, then the cage or the pad, then the petticoats, then the dress.
  - Cut the skirt in gores so that it opens in a bell over the cage.
  - Draw the cloth up at the back into puffs and swags over the bustle.
  - Trim the hem in rows of flounces, braid or pleats.
  - Add gloves, a bonnet or a small hat, and a parasol.

## Construction

- **Methods:** cage of steel hoops on tapes · bustle pad of horsehair and wire · boned and lined fitted bodice · skirt cut in gores · flounces and ruching sewn by hand and machine · toile fitted on the client
- **Closures:** Hooks and eyes down the front of the bodice; the skirt closes at the back with hooks and tapes.
- **Details:** A pointed waist, a bertha collar on the ball gown, ribbon bows, rows of braid, a draped overskirt, and a train for evening.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** synthetic-dyed · glazed · matte · embroidered
- **Pattern:** Plain strong colour, striped or shot silk, and rows of trim; a ball gown carries tulle and flowers.
- **Gloss:** Taffeta and satin at 35–65 GU; velvet at 8–15 GU.
- **Wear:** Silk splits along the fold and the weight of the skirt strains the seams; the dress is re-trimmed and re-cut with each change of line.

### Spring steel hoop (technology)

A frame of flat steel hoops on tapes that holds a skirt out in a wide bell, in place of many layers of horsehair and cotton petticoats. Origin: fashion, Paris and London, c. 1856.

- Roll thin steel into springy hoops and cover them with cotton braid.
- Hang the hoops in a cone from a waist band on tapes, from small at the top to wide at the hem.
- Put a thin petticoat over the cage, and then the dress.

### Toile fitting (skill)

A trial garment in plain calico, fitted and pinned on the client or on a stand before the real cloth is cut. Origin: fashion, Paris, France, c. 1858.

- Cut the toile from calico of the same weight as the final cloth.
- Pin and mark the changes on the body, then take the toile apart and use it as the pattern.
- Fit again in the real cloth two or three times before the hand finish.

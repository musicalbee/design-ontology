# Yixing zisha teapot

> A teapot for steeped tea needs no glaze: a dense, porous clay keeps the tea hot and the taste pure, and the pot improves with use.

Small unglazed teapots in the red-brown stoneware of Yixing. The potter builds them by hand, and the porous wall takes a patina from the tea that it holds.

**Period:** 1500–1850 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Zisha teapot, Purple clay teapot, Yixing ware, Red stoneware teapot

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Yixing zisha teapot, you MUST:

1. Leave the clay bare, so that the wall can take up the oil and the colour of the tea.
2. Build the wall by hand from slabs and strips, 2 to 4 mm thick.
3. Make the lid fit the body so well that a closed spout makes the lid hold when the pot is turned over.
4. Make one pot hold 100 to 300 ml, for one drinker or a small group.
5. Choose one form for one clay: a round pot for smooth clay, a square pot for coarse clay.
6. Cut calligraphy or a poem into the wall, and stamp the maker's seal under the lid and on the base.
7. Fire the pot at 1100 to 1200 °C so that the body is dense but still takes water.
8. Use one clay for one kind of tea, and do not wash it with soap.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** A small teapot with a short spout, a strap handle or a side handle, and a lid with a knob. Forms follow nature or geometry: pumpkin, bamboo, lotus, a plain sphere or a square.
- **Profile:** A round or faceted body on a flat base. The spout rises from the shoulder to the height of the rim, and the lid sits flush with the mouth.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat, bare base with a small ring or three short feet; a flange inside the mouth holds the lid and the rim is cut square.
- **Proportion:** Height 6 to 12 cm and capacity 100 to 300 ml. The spout, the handle and the knob lie on one axis, and the wall is 2 to 4 mm thick.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the pot small and the wall thin.
  - Align the spout mouth, the rim and the handle top on one level line.
  - Fit the lid so tightly that it needs no glaze to seal.
  - Stamp the seal of the maker inside the lid and under the base.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** red-stoneware
- **Secondary:** stoneware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · earthenware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use zisha, an iron-rich clay from the hills of Yixing. Fire it to a dense, unglazed body. Keep porcelain and glazed bodies out of the pot.

## Forming

- **Methods:** pounding the clay into flat slabs and strips · wrapping a slab round a wooden form to make the body · adding the spout, handle and lid as separate parts · smoothing the wall with a steel scraper and a bamboo knife · polishing with a smooth bone or a damp cloth
- **Joins:** The spout and the handle are luted with slip on a cut and scored seat; the lid is cut from the body and ground to fit.
- **Details:** Seams are hidden, the lid is ground to a perfect fit, and the maker stamps a seal on the base and under the lid.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** bare-clay
- **Decorations:** undecorated · incised · sprigging · relief-moulding
- **Pattern:** Bare clay in purple-brown, red or buff; an incised poem or a bamboo branch, or a sprigged plum blossom on the wall.
- **Gloss:** Matt to soft satin, about 2 to 25 GU; hand and tea polish the surface over the years.
- **Wear:** The wall darkens and shines where hands hold it and where tea touches it. Tea oil fills the pores.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Earth tones from one clay: purple-brown, red-brown, buff and a rare dark green. Each pot keeps one tone.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A dragon kiln on a hill slope for the pots in saggars, and later a small bottle kiln; fired with wood.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** air drying for several days · stacking each pot with its lid in a saggar · oxidation firing with wood · slow cooling in the closed kiln
- **Scale:** A craft of small workshops in the town of Yixing; a master made one pot in several days, and the best pots are single pieces.
- **Signs:** Small colour changes where the flame touched the clay, a seal on the base, and fine lines where the slabs were joined.

### Zisha slab building (skill)

A hand method to build a thin-walled teapot from beaten slabs and strips of zisha clay, with no wheel and no mould. Origin: ceramics, Yixing, Jiangsu, China, c. 1500.

- Beat the clay into a flat slab of 2 to 4 mm.
- Cut a base and a strip for the wall and wrap them round a form.
- Scrape the wall smooth with a steel scraper and a bamboo knife.
- Grind the lid to fit the mouth with no gap.

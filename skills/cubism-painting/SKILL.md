# Cubism

> A picture is an object in its own right, and it shows what a thing is by combining several viewpoints on one flat surface.

A picture that shows an object from several sides at once. Analytic Cubism breaks the subject into faceted planes in a narrow brown-grey key; Synthetic Cubism rebuilds it from flat shapes, pasted paper and brighter colour.

**Period:** 1907–1920s · **Era:** Avant-garde · **Domain:** Painting · **Also called:** Analytic Cubism, Synthetic Cubism

Good for: editorial and cultural poster art, album and book covers, portrait illustration with a modern edge, abstract pattern and textile.

Do not use it when:
- The brief needs a clear, friendly face or a recognisable likeness at a glance.
- The subject needs a soft, warm or calm mood.

## System directives

When making, prompting or art-directing a picture: a painting, an illustration, an image-generation prompt or a cover in Cubism, you MUST:

1. Break the subject into 20–60 planes with mostly straight edges, and show each plane from a different viewpoint.
2. Keep the space shallow: stack planes in 2–4 layers that lean forward and never open a deep perspective.
3. In the analytic phase, limit the palette to 3–4 hues of ochre, grey, brown and olive.
4. Make the contour pass through the form: let edges open, so that figure and ground exchange places.
5. Set a grid of verticals and horizontals as the armature, and tilt the planes 5–20° from it.
6. In the synthetic phase, use flat shapes in solid colour, and add real texture: pasted paper, sand, a stencilled letter.
7. Choose a plain, close subject that stands on a table: a guitar, a bottle, a glass, a newspaper, a head.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** Deep single-point perspective with a receding floor.
- **NEVER:** Smooth modelling that turns a form as one solid volume.
- **NEVER:** Soft atmospheric edges and blended colour.
- **NEVER:** A single viewpoint and a full, undistorted face.
- **NEVER:** Strong cast shadows from one light source.
- **NEVER:** Sentimental or narrative subjects that need a story.

## Composition

- **Layout:** A central, pyramidal mass of faceted planes that fades out toward the edges, on a hidden grid of verticals and horizontals with the planes tilted 5–20° off it.
- **Focal point:** The densest cluster of small planes and the strongest value contrast, within the centre 40% of the canvas.
- **Format:** A vertical canvas of 4:5 to 2:3, or an oval; the subject floats inside with a 10–15% margin of lighter ground.

**Principles**

| Principle | Rule |
|---|---|
| rhythm | Repeat the same angled facet 10–20 times, with each repeat shifted or rotated by 5–20°. |
| layering-depth | Compress depth to 2–4 shallow planes with no more than 10% value change between neighbours. |
| focal-point | Concentrate detail and contrast at the centre; let the facets thin out toward the edges. |
| unity-variety | Hold the key to 3–4 hues and one value range; vary the facet size from 10 to 100mm. |
| balance | Balance the mass by an informal offset: a heavy cluster left of centre against open facets on the right. |

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Analytic: a near-monochrome of ochre, grey and brown. Synthetic: flat, brighter local colour, with black, white and one or two primaries.
- **Distribution:** Analytic: one hue family 80–90%, near-white highlights 5–10%, near-black lines under 5%. Synthetic: 4–6 flat colours, each 10–25% of the area.
- **Ground:** A pale ochre or warm grey ground that shows between the planes.

## Brushwork & medium

- **Medium:** Oil paint, thinly bound; later with sand, wallpaper, newsprint and charcoal.
- **Support:** Canvas, medium weave, with a pale ground.
- **Brushwork:** Small, flat strokes of 5–15mm laid in parallel within each plane, and directed along the facet; a tiny, dabbed 'passage' at plane joins.
- **Paint handling:** Thin, dry and matt in the analytic phase; flat, opaque fields with sanded or collaged areas in the synthetic phase.
- **Edges:** Alternating hard and lost edges along the same contour, so that a plane breaks into its neighbour (passage).

## Light & modelling

- **Light:** No single source; light is a pattern of lighter and darker planes spread over the whole object.
- **Modelling:** Each plane has a flat or lightly graded value, 2–3 steps; the solid form is implied by the sequence of planes.
- **Shadow:** Shadow is a dark plane in its own right, not a cast shadow; the darkest value stays inside the figure.
- **Temperature:** One warm-earth family in the analytic phase; in the synthetic phase, warm and cool are set by flat fields side by side.

## Subject

- **Typical:** Still life with guitar, violin, bottle and newspaper; heads and half-figures; café tables; a few landscapes.
- **Figure scale:** The subject fills 60–85% of the canvas and is seen close.
- **Treatment:** Everyday, unheroic objects; an exact likeness is lost, and the identity is kept by a few clues such as a sound-hole, a letter or a scroll.

## Surface

- **Finish:** Matt and dry; sometimes a gloss field against matt planes.
- **Texture:** Flat canvas grain, with sand, paper edge or wood-grain print in the synthetic phase.

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Chronophotography (process)

A series of photographs, or many exposures on one plate, that split a movement into steps and lay the steps side by side. Origin: photography, Palo Alto, 1878.

- Record 12 to 24 frames each second, or 5 to 20 exposures on one plate.
- Hold the camera and the dark ground still; the body moves against a plain field.
- Show each stage of the movement in a form that overlaps its neighbour by 20% to 50%.
- In a painting, repeat a limb or a form along the path of the movement.

### Crystal facet (motif)

A form broken into flat planes that meet at sharp angles, so that it reads as a crystal or a shard. Origin: painting, Horta de Ebro, La Roche-Guyon and Paris, c. 1909.

- Cut each mass into flat planes; most planes meet at angles between 30° and 120°.
- Use one plane size family: 3 to 5 sizes, with no curve.
- Let the light break on each plane, so that adjacent planes differ in tone by one or two steps.

## Organisations

The groups, studios, companies, schools, channels and backers that carried this style.

- **Galerie Kahnweiler** (gallery, Paris, 1907–now), sold the style. The gallery of the dealer Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler. He bought and sold the work of Picasso and Braque as they made Cubism.
- **Jack of Diamonds** (collective, Moscow, 1910–1917), exhibited the style. A Moscow society of painters that brought French Cubism, Cézanne and Russian folk art together. Its first show in 1910 caused a scandal. The radical members left in 1912.
- **Salon des Indépendants** (salon, Paris, 1884–now), exhibited the style. An annual show without a jury. Seurat and Signac showed their dot paintings here, and the Cubists had a room here in 1911.
- **Section d'Or** (collective, Puteaux, 1911–1914), shaped the style. A circle of Cubist painters who met in Puteaux and Courbevoie near Paris. Their name pointed to the golden section and to the long line of classical art. They held one large show in 1912.

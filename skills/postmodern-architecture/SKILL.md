# Postmodernism

> Architecture is communication: a building should carry meaning, memory and irony, and a decorated shed is better than a blank box.

A building that quotes history in a joke and in colour: split pediments, giant keystones, pastel stone and a facade that works as a sign. Venturi, Graves and Johnson used it to answer the blank box of the Modern Movement.

**Period:** 1960s–1990s · **Era:** Postmodern and After · **Domain:** Architecture · **Also called:** Postmodern architecture, PoMo, Historicist modern

Do not use it when:

- The client wants a serious tone without any irony.

## System directives

When designing, describing or rendering buildings and interiors in Postmodernism, you MUST:

1. Quote a historical form in a changed scale, colour or material: a keystone 3 times too large, or a column in plywood.
2. Compose the front as a sign: a flat facade of 2 to 4 distinct layers, with the entrance and the title marked as a symbol.
3. Split and layer the classical elements: a broken pediment, a cut column, a loggia that is only a picture of a loggia.
4. Paint the building: use 3 to 5 flat pastel colours in large fields on stone, render and tile.
5. Combine two or more codes in one front, popular and learned, and let the contradiction show.
6. Make the entrance a stage: a tall arch or portal of 6 to 10m, with a bright lobby behind it.

## Guardrails

Each line below breaks the style. Check your output against the list before you return it.

- **NEVER:** Blank, unadorned glass or concrete boxes.
- **NEVER:** Faithful copies of a historical style with no irony.
- **NEVER:** A single material and a single colour over the whole front.
- **NEVER:** Structure shown as the main image of the facade.
- **NEVER:** Rigid, purist geometry with no historical quotation.
- **NEVER:** Facades with no sign, title or marked entrance.

## Massing & form

- **Form:** A compact block or tower with a symbolic front, a gabled or arched top, and cut-away volumes and loggias.
- **Proportion:** Blocks of 1:1 to 1:3; a tower has a tripartite division of base, shaft and top, in a ratio of about 1:3:1.
- **Roof:** A broken pediment, a gable, a pyramid or a pitched top that serves as the logo of the building.
- **Relation to ground:** A heavy base of stone or painted render, 1 to 2 storeys, and a stage-like entrance with steps and a portal.

## Structure

- **System:** A concrete or steel frame hidden behind stone, stucco, tile or metal cladding.
- **Span and module:** Standard office grids of 7.5 to 9m, hidden behind facades composed on a different, symbolic module.
- **Expression:** The structure is not expressed. The facade is a cladding layer; its pattern is independent of the frame.

## Materials

- **Primary:** painted stucco and render · pink and grey granite · ceramic tile · stone veneer
- **Secondary:** glass block · laminate · coloured metal panels · terrazzo
- **Finish:** Smooth, bright and flat colour; surfaces are painted, tiled or polished, and read as a graphic layer.

## Facade

- **Composition:** A flat, layered front divided into base, middle and top, with oversize historical symbols and one clear entrance.
- **Openings:** Square and arched windows in the middle layer, with a keystone or a surround; the window-to-wall ratio is 20 to 40%.
- **Solid to void:** Solid leads: 60–80% opaque, painted in fields.

## Light & space

- **Daylight:** Daylight is even; in the atrium and lobby, skylights and coloured light turn the room into a stage.
- **Spatial sequence:** A staged route: portal, then a high lobby or atrium, then rooms that quote a street or a piazza.
- **Interior character:** Terrazzo floors, painted arches, laminate with graphic patterns, and a mix of historical furniture and bold new pieces.

## Ornament

- **Attitude:** Ornament is back as quotation and joke: it tells you what the building is and then laughs at it.
- **Details:** oversize keystones · broken pediments · painted pilasters · pastel tile bands · lettering on the facade · cut columns

### Arch (motif)

A curved head over an opening that carries load as compression; inside, a soft frame for a door, a mirror or a niche. Origin: architecture, Rome, c. 200 BCE.

- Use a semicircular head: the rise is half the span.
- Keep the ratio of height to width of the opening at 1.6:1 to 2:1.
- In a room, repeat the arch in 2 or 3 places, for example a door, a mirror and a niche, and no more.

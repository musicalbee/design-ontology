# Porcelain insulator

> A wire needs a dry, hard, glazed surface between it and the earth, so the clay body is dense and the shape is a stack of rain skirts.

Glazed porcelain that holds a live wire away from a pole or a tower. The shape follows the electric field and the rain: a stack of skirts, a brown or grey glaze, and a cement joint to a metal cap.

**Period:** 1850 to now · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Electrical porcelain, Ceramic line insulator, Telegraph and power insulator

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Porcelain insulator, you MUST:

1. Fire a dense body with water absorption below 0.5 %, so that no moisture enters the wall.
2. Glaze every outer surface 0.3 to 0.5 mm thick, in brown or light grey, to shed rain and dirt.
3. Shape the outer face as 2 to 4 skirts that overhang, so that the inner face stays dry in rain.
4. Grow the creepage path with the voltage: about 20 to 25 mm of surface for each kV of line voltage.
5. Leave a ring of the glaze off the cement seat and coat it with sand, so that the cement holds.
6. Make a suspension disc 146 mm across and 254 mm apart in a string, so that parts fit each other.
7. Stamp the maker, the year and the load on the shoulder in raised or incised letters.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Bell-shaped pin insulators for a cross-arm, stacked disc units for a string, solid post insulators, and bushings with a stack of skirts.
- **Profile:** A smooth dome at the top, then 2 to 4 skirts that flare out and curl under, with a deep groove at the neck for the wire.
- **Foot and rim:** A threaded socket or a sanded cement seat at the foot, and a rolled, thick rim on each skirt so that it does not chip.
- **Proportion:** Pin insulator 100 to 300 mm tall, wall 10 to 25 mm thick; each skirt reaches out 25 to 40 mm past the one above.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the skirt rim thick and round: a thin edge chips in transport.
  - Keep the wall the same thickness all round, so that it fires without cracks.
  - Put the wire groove at the top or neck, on a clean glaze.
  - Leave a sanded, unglazed band where the metal cap is cemented.

## Forming

- **Methods:** wet-process throwing and turning on a lathe · jiggering of disc units · dry pressing of small parts · leather-hard turning and joining of units · cement assembly of cap and pin
- **Joins:** A cast iron cap and a steel pin are cemented to the sanded porcelain; large units join from 2 or 3 fired pieces with cement.
- **Details:** A stamped mark of the maker on the shoulder, a thin ring where the glaze stops, and three small spur marks on the foot.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** iron-brown-glaze · clear-feldspathic · sanitary-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** undecorated · incised · relief-moulding
- **Pattern:** No pattern. The only marks are the skirts, the raised ribs, the maker's stamp and the load rating in raised letters.
- **Gloss:** High gloss, about 80 to 95 GU, on a smooth, hard glaze with no pinholes.
- **Wear:** Dust and soot dull the glaze in time; the surface stays hard, and a chip shows the white body under a brown glaze.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A restrained brown, grey or white glaze with a sanded band of grey, and a dark metal cap.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal-fired round downdraught kiln, later a gas or electric tunnel kiln, with saggars for the large units.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** dry the unit slowly for days · dip in glaze · fire in one pass at 1250 to 1400 °C · sand the cement seat · assemble the cap and pin with cement · test with high voltage
- **Scale:** A factory with tunnel kilns; a long string for a 400 kV line holds some 20 to 30 discs.
- **Signs:** A thin glaze ring at the foot, kiln stilt marks, a maker stamp and a test mark; a small pinhole or a sag shows a reject.

### Suspension disc insulator (technology)

A porcelain disc with an iron cap and a steel pin. A string of such discs, each linked to the next, hangs a power line from a tower and grows with the voltage. Origin: industrial design, United States, c. 1906.

- Fire a porcelain disc about 255 mm across with a deep rain skirt on the lower face.
- Cement a cast iron cap on top and a steel pin in the hollow of the disc.
- Link the discs pin to cap in a string, and add one disc for each 10 to 15 kV of line voltage.
- Test each disc with high voltage before use.

# Istoriato maiolica

> A dish can be a painting: the whole surface becomes a scene of myth, scripture or history, painted in many colours on white tin glaze.

Italian tin-glazed earthenware painted with a whole story, called istoriato. Painters copied scenes from prints and fresco and filled the full surface of a dish with figures in colour.

**Period:** 1500–1600 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Istoriato, Italian narrative maiolica, Renaissance maiolica

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Istoriato maiolica, you MUST:

1. Paint the story over the full face of the dish, with no border or a narrow one.
2. Copy the figures and the composition from a print, a fresco or a drawing.
3. Paint on the raw, dry tin glaze with oxides, in one firing, and so make no change.
4. Use a rich palette: blue, yellow, orange, green, manganese purple and white relief.
5. Place the figures in the foreground, and a landscape, a sky and a small building behind.
6. Fire at 950 to 1000 °C, then add lustre in a third firing for the richest pieces.
7. Write a short title or the maker's mark on the back, or on a banner in the scene.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Large plates, shallow bowls, chargers, tazze (footed dishes), and ewers for display and gifts.
- **Profile:** A dish has a wide, shallow well and a low, flat rim, so that the picture reads like a panel.
- **Foot and rim:** A low foot ring with a hole for hanging, and a slightly raised rim.
- **Proportion:** Plate diameter 25 to 45 cm, a wall 5 to 8 mm thick, and a picture that fills about 90 per cent of the face.
- **Rules:**
  - Keep the surface flat and wide, so the picture is not broken by the form.
  - Paint the picture to the edge, with only a thin band at the rim.
  - Coat the back with a clear lead glaze and mark it with a title or a date.
  - Make a tazza with a stem and a wide shallow top for the display of a scene.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a fine, pale buff earthenware that takes a thick, white tin glaze. Prepare the clay to be smooth, so the painting is clean.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the wheel · turning the foot · press moulding of tazze · painting on raw tin glaze · luting of handles
- **Joins:** A tazza is thrown in two parts, the bowl and the stem, and joined with slip.
- **Details:** A pierced foot ring, a clear lead glaze on the back, an inscription or a signature, and a faint pounced line under the figures.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** istoriato · lustre · underglaze-polychrome
- **Pattern:** A single story that fills the plate: a battle, a myth or a Biblical scene, with figures, a landscape and a sky.
- **Gloss:** Glossy tin glaze, about 70 to 85 GU; a lustre adds a gold or ruby sheen to a few pieces.
- **Wear:** The glaze may craze and chip at the rim; the paint stays fresh and bright under the glass.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A full range of oxide colours on a white tin glaze, with blue and yellow as the base for most of the picture.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired kiln for the biscuit and the glaze, and a small muffle kiln for the lustre.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing · dipping in tin glaze · painting on raw glaze · glaze firing at about 980 °C · lustre firing for rich pieces
- **Scale:** Workshops in Urbino and Faenza made service sets for the courts; one painter painted a plate in a few days.
- **Signs:** A thick milk-white glaze, bright colour with a faint blur, a title on the back and three stilt marks.

### Tin glazing (process)

A pot is dipped in a glaze with tin oxide in it. The glaze dries to a white, opaque coat on which a potter paints with oxides before the glaze firing. Origin: ceramics, Basra, Iraq, c. 800.

- Fire the pot once to a biscuit at about 950 °C.
- Dip the biscuit in a bath of glaze with 5 to 10 % tin oxide, so that the coat is about 0.5 mm thick.
- Paint on the dry raw glaze with cobalt, copper, manganese or antimony.
- Fire the glaze at about 1000 °C, so that the colours sink into the glaze.

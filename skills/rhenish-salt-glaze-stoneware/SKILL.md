# Rhenish salt-glaze stoneware

> A dense stoneware pot needs no lining, because a handful of salt thrown into the hot kiln gives it a skin that keeps out water and does not taste of lead.

Hard grey stoneware that the potters of the Rhineland fired to about 1250 °C and glazed with salt. The jugs, bottles and storage jars are tough, waterproof and ready for the cellar.

**Period:** 1400–1700 · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Rhenish stoneware, Steinzeug, Bartmann jugs and Westerwald ware

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Rhenish salt-glaze stoneware, you MUST:

1. Throw the body of a local stoneware clay and fire it to 1200 to 1300 °C until it does not take up water.
2. Throw rock salt into the firebox at the peak of the firing; the vapour forms a glaze 0.1 mm thick with an orange-peel texture.
3. Press a face mask, a coat of arms or a rosette in relief on the neck or the belly of a jug.
4. Keep the neck narrow and the belly round, so that the jug holds 1 to 5 litres and pours well.
5. Pull a strap handle and fix it with slip at the neck and at the belly.
6. Colour the surface in the fire: tan to brown with iron in the clay; after 1590 add cobalt blue and manganese purple in the Westerwald.
7. Stack the pots in the kiln with a gap of 2 to 3 cm, so that the salt vapour reaches every face.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Bartmann jugs with a bearded face on the neck, round-bellied bottles, tall storage jars, cylindrical tankards and wide mugs.
- **Profile:** A narrow neck on a round belly that swells to the lower third; the body narrows to a flat base.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat base cut off the wheel with wire, with a frilled or pinched foot in some Siegburg work; a plain rolled rim with a lip for the pour.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 40 cm; the wall is 4 to 7 mm thick, which is thin for stoneware.
- **Rules:**
  - Put the face mask on the neck of a jug, and let the beard fall to the shoulder.
  - Keep the belly round and the handle a simple strap.
  - Leave the base unglazed or lightly salted.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** stoneware
- **Secondary:** silver
- **Avoid:** earthenware · porcelain · creamware
- **Rule:** Use a plastic grey or white-burning clay that vitrifies at 1200 °C. Keep earthenware and porcelain out: a salt glaze only forms on a dense, silica-rich stoneware. Silver is only for lids and mounts.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the kick wheel · pulling strap handles · press moulding of relief masks and medallions · applying sprigged ornament with slip · turning the foot when leather-hard
- **Joins:** Handles are luted with slip at the neck and the belly; sprigs are pressed on a wet face and smoothed at the edge.
- **Details:** Throwing rings on the belly, a thin clay slip wash on the neck, and an orange-peel skin where the salt vapour settled.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** salt-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** sprigging · relief-moulding · incised · underglaze-cobalt
- **Pattern:** Bearded face masks, coats of arms, rosettes and oak-leaf medallions in relief; in the Westerwald, incised scrolls filled with cobalt blue and manganese purple.
- **Gloss:** A semi-gloss skin of about 20 to 45 GU, with an orange-peel texture.
- **Wear:** The salt skin is hard and does not chip; the colour of the body shows grey, tan or brown at the foot.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Earth colours from the fire: grey, tan and iron brown, with Westerwald cobalt blue and manganese purple as the only strong accents.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired kiln of 2 to 4 cubic metres; rock salt is thrown in through ports in the wall.
- **Atmosphere:** salt-or-soda
- **Processes:** bisque-dry the pots in the open air · stack the pots in the kiln · raise the temperature to about 1250 °C · throw rock salt through the ports · close the kiln and let it cool for 2 days
- **Scale:** By 1500 more than 100 potters worked in Siegburg and Raeren; Frechen jugs went to London and the Low Countries by the barrel.
- **Signs:** Orange-peel texture, a darker brown flash where the salt hit the wall, and a dry unglazed base.

### Salt glazing (process)

Rock salt is thrown into a kiln at stoneware heat. The salt vapour reacts with the clay and forms a thin hard glaze with an orange-peel texture. Origin: ceramics, Rhineland, Germany, c. 1400.

- Fire the stoneware to 1200 to 1300 °C.
- Throw 1 to 2 kg of rock salt for each cubic metre through ports in the kiln wall.
- Seal the ports and let the vapour settle for 20 to 30 minutes.
- Keep the wall 4 mm or more thick, so that the pot does not crack in the heat shock.

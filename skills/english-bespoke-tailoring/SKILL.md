# English bespoke tailoring

> A man's suit is drafted for one body, built inside with canvas, and shown outside by a plain surface and a line with no fuss.

The cut of the London tailor: a plain, dark suit in fine worsted, drafted for one body and built by hand on hair canvas.

**Period:** 1800–present · **Era:** Revolution and Regency · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Savile Row tailoring, London cut, The English drape

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in English bespoke tailoring, you MUST:

1. Draft the pattern from 20 or more measures, and cut it by hand from a single length of cloth.
2. Baste a first fitting, then a forward fitting, then a finished fitting; allow 50 or more hours of work for a suit.
3. Build the chest on a canvas of horsehair and wool, pad-stitched by hand to hold the roll of the lapel.
4. Cut the coat with a clean chest and a gentle drape, and a waist that is lightly suppressed.
5. Set the sleeve head with a small roped or natural edge; sew the buttonholes by hand.
6. Choose a dark, plain cloth of 280 to 400 g per metre: navy, charcoal, grey.
7. Keep the trousers high at the waist, with pleats, a straight leg and a turn-up.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A long, balanced line: a clean chest, a soft shoulder, a lightly shaped waist and a straight leg.
- **Line:** The lapel rolls in one curve to the first button; the back hangs without a ripple; the trouser falls straight to a light break.
- **Fit:** Close on the chest and the shoulder, with room at the arm and the seat, so that the wearer can move.
- **Proportion:** Jacket length covers the seat; lapel about 8 to 10 cm; trouser rise about 30 cm; sleeve shows 1 cm of shirt cuff.
- **Rules:**
  - Fit the shoulder first; the rest of the coat hangs from it.
  - Roll the lapel by hand, and do not press it flat.
  - Cut the back with a centre seam and a vent or two side vents, so that it sits flat.
  - Pair a dark coat with a plain waistcoat or a light shirt; keep the colour low.
  - Match the stripe or the check at each seam.

## Fabrics & materials

- **Primary:** worsted-suiting · flannel · tweed · serge
- **Secondary:** canvas · silk-twill · linen-cloth · gabardine · houndstooth · harris-tweed · poplin
- **Avoid:** spandex-jersey · polar-fleece · denim · jersey
- **Rule:** Choose a fine wool with a close twill: worsted for town, flannel and tweed for the country, canvas inside and silk twill for the lining.

## Construction

- **Methods:** pattern drafted to the measures of one client · baste fitting and forward fitting · hair canvas pad-stitched to the chest · hand-sewn buttonholes · hand-felled collar and lining · shoulder and sleeve head set by hand
- **Closures:** A two- or three-button front in horn or corozo; four buttons at the sleeve that open; a fly front on the trousers.
- **Details:** Notch or peak lapel, flap pockets, a ticket pocket, a vent or two, cuff buttons that open, and a lining in silk twill.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** matte · napped · fulled
- **Pattern:** Plain cloth, or a discreet stripe, check or herringbone; the pattern is matched at each seam.
- **Gloss:** Worsted at 3–8 GU; the cloth stays matte, with a soft face from the press.
- **Wear:** A good suit improves with wear: the canvas takes the shape of the body, and the tailor can let out, take in and re-press.

### Hand-canvassed tailoring (skill)

The tailor builds the chest of a coat from layers of hair canvas, shaped and stitched by hand, so that the coat holds its roll and fits the body. Origin: fashion, London, England, c. 1800.

- Cut the canvas from horsehair and wool cloth, and shape it with the iron.
- Pad-stitch the canvas to the cloth of the chest with small diagonal stitches, so that the lapel rolls.
- Sew the shoulder, the collar and the lining by hand, and press the shape into the coat.

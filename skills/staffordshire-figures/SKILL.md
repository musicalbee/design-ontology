# Staffordshire figures

> A figure for every mantelpiece comes from a plaster mould and a clear glaze: flat, bold and cheap, it puts a hero, a king or a dog in a poor man's house.

Earthenware figures from Staffordshire: small folk figures, then Victorian flatbacks of heroes, royals and dogs. A lead glaze and bold enamel colours make a cheap mantelpiece figure for the home.

**Period:** 1745–1900 · **Era:** The porcelain race · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Staffordshire pottery figures, Staffordshire flatbacks, Astbury-Whieldon figures, Staffordshire dogs

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Staffordshire figures, you MUST:

1. Press the figure in a two-piece plaster mould, and use a flat back for the flatback form.
2. Use a white or cream earthenware, and cover it with a clear lead glaze.
3. Paint with bold, flat colours: blue, red, green, yellow and black, with gilt on the details.
4. Make the figure 15 to 40 cm high, with a simple base that carries a name in relief or in gilt.
5. Choose a subject for the public: a royal, a soldier, a preacher, a murderer, a pair of spaniels.
6. Pair the figures, as a left and a right, to flank the clock on the mantelpiece.
7. Give a spaniel a white coat with red or black patches, a gilt chain and a padlock.
8. Fire the enamel colours in a third firing at about 700 °C.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Figures of people, animals and groups, often in pairs; the flatback is flat behind and meant to stand against a wall.
- **Profile:** A simple, rounded outline with a short body and a large head; a pair of spaniels sits face to face with raised chests.
- **Foot and rim:** A low, flat or scrolled base; the flatback has a flat, unglazed or thinly glazed back and a small vent hole.
- **Proportion:** Height 15 to 40 cm; the wall is 4 to 6 mm thick, and the head is about one fifth of the height.
- **Rules:**
  - Press the front in a mould and keep the back flat for the flatback.
  - Make the left and right figures a pair.
  - Keep the modelling simple, with bold folds and a clear outline.
  - Name the subject on the base.
  - Use clear, flat enamel colours with gilt for details.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware · creamware · pearlware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · european-hard-paste · parian · stoneware
- **Rule:** Use a white to cream earthenware that is cheap, light and porous. Keep the walls thick enough for rough use, and keep porcelain out.

## Forming

- **Methods:** press moulding in plaster moulds · slip casting in plaster moulds · hand modelling of small details · sprigging of applied parts · hand painting in enamel colours
- **Joins:** Arms, heads and props are pressed apart and fixed with slip. The seam is cleaned and the glaze covers it.
- **Details:** A small vent hole in the back or in the base, a visible mould seam on the side, and a thin line of gilt on the edge of the base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** lead-glaze · coloured-gloss
- **Decorations:** overglaze-enamel · underglaze-polychrome · gilding · slip-trailing
- **Pattern:** Flat blocks of colour with black outline: a red coat, a blue skirt, a white face. A dog has a gilt chain and a padlock on the chest.
- **Gloss:** Gloss, about 70 to 90 GU on the lead glaze; the enamel colours are a little lower.
- **Wear:** The enamel colours flake and wear at the edges. The lead glaze crazes in a fine net, and chips show the white clay.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A white glaze ground with strong flat colours: cobalt blue, iron red, copper green, yellow and black, with gold on the details.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal-fired bottle oven, with a first firing for the biscuit and a second for the glaze, then a muffle kiln for the enamels.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 1100 °C · dipping in lead glaze · glost firing at about 1000 °C · enamel firing at about 700 °C · gilding firing
- **Scale:** A cottage industry of many small potteries in Burslem, Tunstall and Hanley, with output of thousands of figures a week in the 1850s.
- **Signs:** A mould seam, a vent hole in the back, a thin glaze and a slightly blurred enamel line.

### Slip casting (process)

Liquid clay is poured into a plaster mould. The plaster takes water from the clay and a wall forms on the inside of the mould. Origin: ceramics, Staffordshire, England, c. 1745.

- Make a plaster mould in two or more parts.
- Pour slip into the mould and wait 10 to 20 minutes for a wall of 3 to 5 mm.
- Pour out the rest of the slip.
- Open the mould when the clay is leather-hard and fettle the seam.

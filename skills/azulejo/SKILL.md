# Azulejo

> A wall becomes a picture when many square tiles are painted as one scene on a white tin glaze.

Painted tin-glazed tiles that cover the walls of churches, palaces and streets in Portugal and Spain. A painter works in blue or in many colours on a white glaze, and one scene runs across many tiles.

**Period:** 1560–present · **Era:** Blue and white · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Portuguese tile, Spanish painted tile, Azulejos

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Azulejo, you MUST:

1. Paint the scene on the raw tin glaze, so that each stroke dries at once and cannot be changed.
2. Plan the scene across a grid of square tiles, about 13 to 15 cm each, and number the tiles on the back.
3. Use cobalt blue alone for narrative panels; use many colours for pattern carpets.
4. Repeat a pattern tile in a 2 by 2 or 4 by 4 group to make a carpet of stars and flowers.
5. Frame a large panel with painted volutes, shells, putti and a cornice.
6. Cover the wall to the ceiling, or to a dado of about 1.5 m, with no bare patch.
7. Keep the tile body thin and the glaze thick, about 0.5 mm.
8. Use a bold blue and yellow in the early pattern tiles and a soft blue in the 18th-century panels.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Square tiles in panels for church naves, cloisters, palace walls, stairs, kitchens and street fronts; frieze tiles and corner tiles.
- **Profile:** A flat face with a soft swell of the thick glaze; the tile edge is slightly rounded and not cut square.
- **Foot and rim:** The back is rough and unglazed with ribs for the mortar; the edge of a panel runs into painted frames or a stone moulding.
- **Proportion:** A tile is about 13 to 15 cm square and 8 to 12 mm thick; a panel has 100 or more tiles.
- **Rules:**
  - Paint across the joints, not tile by tile.
  - Keep the blue to one or two tones, from a pale wash to a deep line.
  - Close each panel with a painted frame or a border.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** terracotta
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · porcelain-stoneware
- **Rule:** Use a buff or red earthenware that fires soft and takes a thick tin glaze. Keep dense bodies out: the glaze must stay thick and soft.

## Forming

- **Methods:** pressing the tile in a wooden mould · drying the tile in the shade · pricking a paper cartoon and pouncing the pattern · painting on the raw glaze · numbering the tiles on the back
- **Joins:** Tiles are set in lime mortar, with joints of 1 to 2 mm, and the panel is set from the top down.
- **Details:** Pounce dots in the painted line, a soft blur of the blue at the edge, small chips at the corners and the mark of the pottery on the back.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze
- **Decorations:** underglaze-cobalt · underglaze-polychrome · cuerda-seca · transfer-print
- **Pattern:** Blue narrative scenes in baroque frames, carpet patterns of stars and flowers in blue, yellow and green, and later floral and geometric tiles.
- **Gloss:** Glossy and thick, about 60 to 85 GU.
- **Wear:** The glaze chips at the corners and shows the buff body; the blue stays strong and the white turns a little grey.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Cobalt blue on a white tin glaze; early and folk work adds manganese purple, copper green and antimony yellow.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired kiln; the tiles stand on edge in stacks, with stilts between them.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** a biscuit firing of the pressed tile · dipping in tin glaze · painting on the raw glaze · a second firing for the glaze
- **Scale:** Lisbon workshops made whole church walls in a year; a panel used hundreds of tiles and two or three painters.
- **Signs:** Three stilt marks on the face or edge, numbers on the back, and a soft blue blur where the cobalt sank into the glaze.

### Cuerda seca (process)

A line of grease and manganese oxide is drawn round each colour field on an unfired tile, so that the coloured glazes do not run into each other. The line fires to a matt dark edge. Origin: ceramics, Iran and Spain, c. 1400.

- Draw the design on the biscuit tile.
- Trace each outline with a mix of grease and manganese oxide.
- Fill each field with a different glaze.
- Fire the tile so that the grease burns out and leaves a dark, matt line.

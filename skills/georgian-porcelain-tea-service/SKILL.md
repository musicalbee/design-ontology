# Georgian porcelain tea service

> English potters make a full tea table in porcelain for the new tea drinkers: a set of matched pieces, with a Chinese or a European look.

The first English porcelain tea services. A tea set of handleless bowls, a pot, jugs and slop bowls in soft-paste or bone china, with blue painting, enamel flowers and gilt rims.

**Period:** 1745–1830 · **Era:** The porcelain race · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** English soft-paste tea service, Georgian tea set, Bow and Worcester tea ware, English porcelain tea service

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Georgian porcelain tea service, you MUST:

1. Make a set of 12 to 40 pieces in one body and one pattern.
2. Make the tea bowls handleless at first and add handles after about 1790.
3. Paint flowers, birds and Chinese figures in blue or in enamel colours.
4. Pick one border for the whole set: gilt line, blue band or scalloped edge.
5. Add gilding to the rim, the handle and the knob of the pot.
6. Make the pot rounded on a low foot, with a straight or curved spout and a knob lid.
7. Use the soft glassy glaze that pools in the corners and shows the tint of the body.
8. Copy Chinese and Meissen models first, and then design a new English form.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** A tea service: teapot and stand, handleless tea bowls and saucers, a slop bowl, a milk jug, a sugar bowl with a lid and a bread plate. A caddy and a spoon tray may be added.
- **Profile:** A pot has a round or a barrel body with a curved spout and a loop handle. A tea bowl has a wide, shallow shape on a low foot; a saucer is deep.
- **Foot and rim:** A narrow foot ring on every piece; the rim is round or scalloped, with a thin line of gilding or blue.
- **Proportion:** A tea bowl is 7 to 9 cm wide and 4 to 5 cm high; the wall is 1.5 to 3 mm thick and the bowl is light.
- **Rules:** Match the shapes and the pattern across the set. · Keep the wall thin and the foot narrow. · Add one gilt line to each rim and handle. · Add a lid with a flower or a knob to every lidded piece.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** soft-paste-porcelain · bone-china
- **Secondary:** silver
- **Avoid:** stoneware · red-stoneware · terracotta
- **Rule:** Use soft-paste porcelain in the early years, and bone china from 1748. Keep a stoneware body out: a thin, pale wall that shows light is the aim.

## Forming

- **Methods:** press moulding in plaster moulds · throwing and turning tea bowls · slip casting of pots and jugs · luting handles and spouts with slip · painting in underglaze blue and in enamel · gilding with gold and honey
- **Joins:** Spouts, handles and knobs are cast or pressed in moulds and fixed with slip; the lid is made with the pot to fit.
- **Details:** Moulded leaf and flower shapes, a painted border, a gilt line, and a mark of the maker under the foot.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** soft-paste-glaze · clear-feldspathic
- **Decorations:** underglaze-cobalt · overglaze-enamel · gilding · transfer-print · sprigging · hand-banding · relief-moulding
- **Pattern:** Chinese-style flowers and figures in blue; European sprays, birds and insects in enamel; borders in gold or in blue bands and scallops.
- **Gloss:** Soft gloss to gloss, about 70 to 95 GU; soft-paste glaze looks glassy and pools in the corners.
- **Wear:** Gilding rubs away first on rims and handles; the glaze shows fine crazing in old soft-paste pieces.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A coal- or wood-fired bottle kiln for the biscuit and the glaze, and a small muffle kiln for the enamel and the gold.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 1250 °C for bone china · dipping in glaze · glaze firing at about 1050 to 1100 °C · painting in enamel colours · enamel firing at about 800 °C · gilding and a last firing at about 750 °C
- **Scale:** Factories in London, Worcester and Staffordshire made services for the new class of tea drinkers, with 30 to 100 workers each.
- **Signs:** A glaze with small black specks, a ring of glaze-free ground under the foot and a thin gilt edge.

## Techniques

These techniques started in one domain and moved into this style. Apply the rules as they are written; the full record is in [`TECHNIQUES.md`](../TECHNIQUES.md).

### Overglaze enamelling (process)

Coloured glass enamels are painted on a glazed and fired piece. A third firing at a low heat fixes them on the glaze. Origin: ceramics, Jingdezhen, Jiangxi, China, c. 1465.

- Fire the piece with a clear glaze.
- Paint the design with enamels of red, green, yellow and blue.
- Fire it again in a small muffle kiln at 750 to 850 °C.
- Add gold in a last low firing, when the design needs it.

### Transfer printing (process)

A design is engraved on a copper plate, printed on thin paper in a ceramic ink and moved from the paper to the glazed or biscuit piece. Origin: ceramics, Liverpool and Worcester, England, c. 1756.

- Engrave the design on a copper plate.
- Print it on thin tissue paper in ceramic ink.
- Press the wet tissue on the piece and rub it down.
- Wash the paper away and fire the piece to fix the ink.

# Mid-century eyewear

> Glasses are a part of the face, and the frame gives the wearer a character.

The glasses and sun glasses of the years after 1937. Frames in celluloid and acetate took the cat-eye, browline and round panto shapes, beside the thin gold-filled aviator. Glasses became a fashion item and not only a medical aid.

**Period:** 1937–1965 · **Era:** Interwar modern · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Cat-eye and browline glasses, 1940s–50s spectacle frames

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Mid-century eyewear, you MUST:

1. Cut the frame in plastic, 3 to 5 mm thick, with a keyhole or a flat bridge.
2. Raise the outer corner of the lens by 10 to 20 degrees for the cat-eye.
3. Join a heavy brow bar of plastic to a thin metal rim for the browline.
4. Choose a tortoiseshell pattern or a solid colour in the plastic.
5. Set rhinestones or metal studs in the upper corner of a ladies' frame.
6. Fix the temples with 5-barrel hinges on a core wire.
7. Make the lens 48 to 54 mm wide.
8. Offer one frame in many colours, so that the wearer owns more than one pair.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A pair of lenses in a frame that sweeps up at the outer edge, or sits heavy on the brow.
- **Line:** A strong top line that follows the brow, and a lower rim that curves away.
- **Fit:** The frame sits on the bridge of the nose, with the temples running straight back over the ears.
- **Proportion:** Lens 48–54 mm wide; bridge 16–22 mm; temple 135–145 mm; cat-eye tip 10–20 degrees above the horizontal.
- **Rules:**
  - Lift the outer corner, so that the eye looks wider and longer.
  - Make the top of the frame heavier than the bottom.
  - Keep the bridge low and the temples slim.
  - Add detail only at the corner: a stud, a stone or a metal tip.

## Fabrics & materials

- **Primary:** acetate-sheet · celluloid · tortoiseshell · yellow-gold
- **Secondary:** stainless-steel · acrylic-glass · rose-gold · paste · brass · calfskin
- **Avoid:** titanium · carbon-fibre · abs-plastic · technical-ceramic
- **Rule:** Use a plastic that polishes to a deep gloss and takes a tortoiseshell pattern, and a thin gold-filled or steel wire for metal frames.

## Construction

- **Methods:** frame cut from laminated acetate sheet · hot bending of the front · 5-barrel hinge on a core wire · rhinestones set in the corner · metal rim joined to a plastic brow bar
- **Closures:** Temple arms on riveted hinges; a nose pad of plastic or of metal.
- **Details:** Rhinestone studs, a keyhole bridge, a metal brow rod and a maker's mark on the temple.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** polished · matte · brushed · rhodium-plated · engraved · cabochon
- **Pattern:** A tortoiseshell mottle, a solid colour or a laminate of two colours: black over pink or clear.
- **Gloss:** Polished plastic at 90–100 GU; brushed steel at 20–35 GU.
- **Wear:** A frame yellows and scratches with age; a clean, glossy frame is the aim, and a collector prizes an unworn pair.

### Layered plastic frame (material)

A spectacle frame cut and polished from sheets of celluloid or acetate, often two or more layers of colour glued together before cutting. Origin: fashion, United States, c. 1880.

- Press the layers of plastic into one sheet, 3 to 5 mm thick.
- Cut the front and the temples with a saw or a router, then tumble and polish them.
- Warm the front and bend it to the curve of the face; fit the hinges with rivets.

## Organisations

The groups, studios, companies, schools, channels and backers that carried this style.

- **Bausch & Lomb optical works** (company, Rochester, New York, 1853–now), launched the style. The optical firm of two German emigrants in Rochester. In 1937 it launched sun glasses with a thin metal frame and a large teardrop lens for pilots.
- **Harper's Bazaar** (magazine, New York, 1867–now), published the style. A fashion magazine. It printed the photographs of Richard Avedon and used Didot type for its name.

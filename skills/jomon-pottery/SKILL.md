# Jōmon pottery

> A pot is a coiled and fired form that carries the marks of its making: the cord, the cut and the clay itself are the ornament.

The oldest pottery of Japan. Hunter-gatherers coiled a coarse clay into deep pots, pressed twisted cord into the wet wall, and fired the pots in an open bonfire.

**Period:** c. 14000–300 BC · **Era:** Ancient clay · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Jomon ware, Cord-marked pottery, Flame-style pottery

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Jōmon pottery, you MUST:

1. Build the pot from clay coils 15 to 25 mm thick and smooth only the inside.
2. Roll twisted cord across the wet outer wall to leave a dense, even texture.
3. Keep the pot deep: the height is one to two times the mouth width.
4. Fire the pot in an open bonfire at 600 to 900 °C, with no kiln and no glaze.
5. Raise the rim of a ceremonial pot into a crown of clay loops, handles and spikes.
6. Divide the surface into bands: cord-marked, incised, and plain zones that alternate.
7. Leave the clay body in its fired colour, from buff to dark brown, with black fire clouds.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** A deep cooking and storage pot with a round or pointed base and a mouth that is as wide as the belly or wider.
- **Profile:** An S-curve or a straight cylinder; flame-style pots narrow at the waist and open into a tall crown at the rim.
- **Foot and rim:** A rounded or small flat base; the rim carries applied loops, curls and four large handles on the ceremonial pots.
- **Proportion:** Height 30 to 60 cm, mouth 25 to 45 cm, and a wall 8 to 20 mm thick.
- **Rules:**
  - Make the base pointed or round for a pot that stands in ash or sand.
  - Build the crown on the rim in four symmetrical groups of loops.
  - Keep the wall thick and uneven; the coil joins stay visible inside.
  - Place the densest ornament at the rim and the shoulder.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Avoid:** porcelain · stoneware · bone-china
- **Rule:** Use a coarse local clay with sand, mica or plant fibre. The body is porous and soft, and it stays dark and uneven after the fire.

## Forming

- **Methods:** coiling · pinching the base · paddle smoothing · cord rolling · clay application
- **Joins:** Coils are pressed together and blended with wet fingers; handles and rim loops are rolled and pressed on with slip.
- **Details:** Cord, rope and shell impressions on the wall, finger-tip marks along the rim, and a dark patch where the fire touched the surface.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** bare-clay
- **Decorations:** cord-impressed · incised · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Twisted cord rolled in bands, then incised curves, spirals and bold ridges; the flame-style rim is a crown of applied clay.
- **Gloss:** Dry matt, below 5 GU; no glaze covers the body.
- **Wear:** The surface is chalky and soft; the cord texture and the fire clouds stay clear after thousands of years.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Earth tones from the clay and the fire only: buff, brown and black, with no added colour.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** An open bonfire or a shallow pit with wood, grass and dry bark as fuel; there is no kiln.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** dry the pot for several days · stack the pots in a pit · cover with fuel · fire in the open for a few hours
- **Scale:** A household or a village made the pots by hand, one at a time, in a few dozen pieces for each season.
- **Signs:** Uneven colour, black fire clouds, a soft chalky wall and visible coil joins.

### Cord marking (process)

Twisted cord or a cord-wrapped stick is rolled or pressed into the wet clay wall. It leaves a dense texture of fine ridges. Origin: ceramics, Japanese archipelago, c. 14000 BCE.

- Twist two strands of plant fibre into a cord.
- Wind the cord round a stick or lay it flat on the wet wall.
- Roll or press it across the wall in rows or bands.
- Dry the pot and fire it in a bonfire.

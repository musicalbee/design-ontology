# British modernist studio ceramics

> A modern pot is a precise form with a quiet surface, where the glaze and the clay do the work that ornament did before.

Fine studio ceramics from London after 1946. Lucie Rie and Hans Coper worked in a small studio and made thin bowls, bottles and sculptural forms with a quiet, textured surface.

**Period:** 1946–1990 · **Era:** Modern ceramics · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Rie and Coper ceramics, Albion Mews pottery, Modern British studio pottery

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in British modernist studio ceramics, you MUST:

1. Throw the form thin and exact: a porcelain wall of 2 to 3 mm and a stoneware wall of 3 to 6 mm.
2. Keep the profile simple: a cylinder, a cone, a sphere or a combination of them.
3. Join two or three thrown parts with a visible rim, to make a form that is not a single throw.
4. Coat the stoneware with a thin manganese or a white slip, and leave it matt and unglazed.
5. Make the glaze pitted, volcanic or thick, in one colour, such as pink, yellow, white or bronze.
6. Scratch a line through a slip or a glaze with a pointed tool, in a pattern of 1 to 2 mm lines.
7. Fire in an electric kiln, in oxidation, at 1200 to 1280 °C.
8. Make a small number of pieces and a small range of forms.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Bottles, shallow and deep bowls, cylinder vases, spade and cup forms, and sculptural vessels with a flat disc or a stem.
- **Profile:** A clear geometric profile: a sphere on a stem, a cone with a flat rim, a wide bowl with a thin, steep wall.
- **Foot and rim:** A very small foot, or a bare disc base; the rim is sharp and thin, or a flat disc that is an essential part of the form.
- **Proportion:** Height 8 to 35 cm; porcelain wall 2 to 3 mm and stoneware wall 3 to 6 mm; the foot is one third of the width or less.
- **Rules:**
  - Make the profile exact, and check it with a template.
  - Join two or more thrown parts for a non-round or a stacked form.
  - Keep the colour to one tone and the surface to one texture.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** stoneware · porcelain
- **Avoid:** earthenware · bone-china · jasperware
- **Rule:** Use a fine white or dark stoneware and a hard porcelain. Do not use an earthenware, which cannot give the thin hard wall.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing in sections · joining of thrown parts · turning and trimming of the foot · scratching with a pointed tool · coating with slip or manganese · electric kiln firing
- **Joins:** Two or three thrown parts are luted with slip and finished in the leather-hard state, to make a clear, firm seam.
- **Details:** A thin rim, a bare disc foot, a pitted or textured glaze, a line scratched in the slip and a small impressed seal.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** matte-glaze · satin-glaze · bare-clay · clear-feldspathic
- **Decorations:** sgraffito · wax-resist · incised · undecorated
- **Pattern:** A single glaze that is pitted, volcanic or thick; a band of fine scratched lines; a matt manganese coat with a pale rim.
- **Gloss:** Matt to satin, 5 to 40 GU; the surface is quiet and not shiny.
- **Wear:** The matt surface marks easily, and the unglazed band shows the finger marks; the porcelain is hard and keeps its edge.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** A very limited palette of one quiet tone for each piece: white, pink, yellow, bronze or black.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** An electric kiln, with an even oxidation.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 1000 °C · coating with slip or glaze · glaze firing to 1200 to 1280 °C · a second firing for a thick or pitted glaze · polishing of the foot
- **Scale:** One small studio of two to four workers, with a few hundred pieces a year.
- **Signs:** A pitted glaze, a faint seam at the join, a scratched line and a pale unglazed band.

### Thrown and assembled form (process)

A potter throws two or more separate parts and joins them at the leather-hard stage. The joined form has a shape that no single throw can make. Origin: ceramics, London, England, c. 1955.

- Throw each part separately, a cylinder, a disc or a bowl.
- Let the parts dry to leather-hard.
- Join them with slip and press them together.
- Turn and smooth the join and the foot.

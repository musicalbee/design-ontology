# Memphis postmodern ceramics

> A vase is a toy, a totem and a joke at the same time: its form is a stack of simple solids, and its surface is a pattern in loud colour.

Ceramic vessels from the Memphis group of Milan after 1981. They combine plain geometric solids with loud colour and patterns of stripes, dots and terrazzo, and they ignore good taste.

**Period:** 1981–1990 · **Era:** Plural and contemporary · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Memphis Milano ceramics, Postmodern ceramic vessels, Italian postmodern pottery

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Memphis postmodern ceramics, you MUST:

1. Build the form from 3 to 5 simple solids: a cone, a sphere, a cylinder, a cube and a ring.
2. Use 4 or more bright colours on one piece, with black and white as colours too.
3. Cover the surface with stripes, dots, zigzags or terrazzo flecks in a regular repeat.
4. Put two patterns on one object, and let them clash.
5. Make the glaze flat and glossy, so that the form and the colour read as one graphic.
6. Name each piece after a place or a myth, and sign it with the name of the designer.
7. Print the pattern by screen print or decal, and fix it in a second firing at 750 to 850 °C.
8. Treat the vessel as a statement, not as a holder: a vase may have no opening that works.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Vases and bottle vases made of stacked solids, striped jars with a lid, shallow bowls with a stand, and sculptural vessels.
- **Profile:** A tall, stepped form with a wide cone, a cylinder and a sphere set one on the other; the outline is sharp and geometric.
- **Foot and rim:** A flat, square or round foot that is part of the pattern; the rim is a clean flat circle or a coloured band.
- **Proportion:** Height 20 to 60 cm; the wall is 4 to 8 mm and the form is often wider at the top than at the base.
- **Rules:** Use simple solids and clear steps. · Align the pattern to the edges of each solid. · Make the colours clash at each step.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware · stoneware
- **Secondary:** porcelain · plastic
- **Avoid:** bone-china · jasperware · raku-clay
- **Rule:** Use a white or light body that takes bright colour: an earthenware or a stoneware for strong shapes, and a porcelain for fine pieces. Plastic parts may form a base.

## Forming

- **Methods:** slip casting in plaster moulds · joining of cast solids · screen printing of patterns on the glazed surface · decal application · glaze spraying in flat colour
- **Joins:** The solids are cast in separate moulds and joined with slip; the seam falls on a colour edge.
- **Details:** Sharp edges between colour fields, a flat even glaze, a printed pattern that is aligned with the solids and a signature mark on the base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** coloured-gloss · matte-glaze · satin-glaze
- **Decorations:** decal · screen-print · hand-banding · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Stripes, dots, zigzags and a speckle that looks like terrazzo, in black and white and bright colour, with a flat colour field between them.
- **Gloss:** Flat gloss in most fields, 70 to 95 GU; some fields are matt.
- **Wear:** A printed pattern can wear at the rim; the glossy glaze scratches, and the colours stay bright.

## Palette

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A gas tunnel kiln for the body and glaze; a small kiln for the decals and the lustre.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** slip casting and joining of the solids · biscuit firing at about 1000 °C · spraying of the flat colour glazes · glaze firing at 1050 to 1180 °C · decal or screen print firing at 750 to 850 °C
- **Scale:** Limited editions of a few hundred or thousand pieces, made by small Italian factories.
- **Signs:** Sharp colour edges, a very regular printed pattern and a designer's mark on the base.

### Screen printing (process)

Ink pushed through a fine mesh with a squeegee; a stencil on the mesh blocks the parts that do not print. Origin: print, Manchester, 1907.

- Stretch a silk or polyester mesh of 40–120 threads a centimetre on a frame.
- Make the stencil by hand cut film or by a photo emulsion exposed through a film positive.
- Pull thick, opaque ink across the screen with a rubber squeegee in one even stroke.
- Print each colour from its own screen and let each layer dry before the next.

# Abstract Expressionist ceramics

> The pot is a place for a gesture: a thrown cylinder can be cut, torn and stacked like paint on a canvas, and the use of the vessel no longer sets the form.

American ceramics after 1954 in which the potter treats the vessel as a sculpture. Peter Voulkos threw large forms, cut, tore and stacked them, and painted them with the gesture of action painting.

**Period:** 1954–1975 · **Era:** Modern ceramics · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Otis group ceramics, Voulkos school, Clay as sculpture

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Abstract Expressionist ceramics, you MUST:

1. Throw large cylinders of 40 to 100 cm, in 3 to 5 parts, and join them while they are soft.
2. Cut, tear, paddle and puncture the thrown wall so that the action stays visible.
3. Stack the parts into a tall, uneven form, with open holes that show the inside.
4. Paint the clay with thick slip and iron in broad gestures, as an action painter paints a canvas.
5. Use thick, raw glazes: black iron, ash and tenmoku, with runs and bare clay patches.
6. Fire in a gas kiln in reduction at 1200 to 1300 °C; accept the cracks and the warps.
7. Leave a vessel opening, a lid or a spout as a sign of the pot, even when the pot cannot hold liquid.
8. Work fast and in a group, and call the studio a place of work, not a pottery.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Large stacked pots, bottles with torn walls, big plates and chargers, lidded jars that are open, and sculptural vessels.
- **Profile:** A tall column of thrown rings with cut openings, a slashed surface, and a plate-like disc that is added at the top.
- **Foot and rim:** A heavy, wide base with a torn edge; the rim is cut or folded, not smooth.
- **Proportion:** Height 40 to 120 cm; the wall is 10 to 25 mm, thick enough to take cutting and paddling.
- **Rules:**
  - Make the form at a scale of the body, not of the hand.
  - Keep the signs of cutting and tearing in the finished surface.
  - Leave one opening in the form, as a link to the pot.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** stoneware
- **Secondary:** raku-clay
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · jasperware
- **Rule:** Use a coarse, grogged stoneware that can be thrown large, torn and stacked without slumping. A raku clay serves for small, fast pieces.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing of large cylinders · cutting and tearing of the wall · paddling and puncturing · stacking and joining of thrown parts · painting with slip and iron · raku firing for small works
- **Joins:** The parts are joined with thick slip and a wet hand, and the join is made part of the surface, not hidden.
- **Details:** Torn edges, finger and tool marks, big cracks, glaze that pools and runs, and bare clay left in patches.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tenmoku · ash-glaze · raku-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** iron-painting · incised · slip-trailing · wax-resist
- **Pattern:** A broad gesture of black iron or thick white slip across a torn surface; a thick ash or tenmoku glaze that runs and breaks over the cuts.
- **Gloss:** Matt to gloss, 10 to 70 GU, with a large variation on one piece.
- **Wear:** The surface is robust; the bare clay patches darken with handling, and raku pieces are porous.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Dark, earthy tones with a few high-contrast marks: black iron, white slip and rust, with raw clay.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A large gas kiln that fires in reduction; a fast raku kiln for small pieces.
- **Atmosphere:** reduction
- **Processes:** throwing and stacking in one session · slow drying under plastic for 3 to 7 days · biscuit firing at about 1000 °C · glaze firing in reduction at 1200 to 1300 °C · raku firing at about 900 °C for small works
- **Scale:** One large piece takes a day to make and a week to dry; a firing holds about 10 big works.
- **Signs:** Large cracks, warps, thick flows of glaze, torn edges and a heavy, uneven base.

### Thrown-form alteration (skill)

A potter throws a plain vessel on the wheel and then bends, pinches, cuts and joins it while it is wet, so that the vessel becomes an animal or a figure. Origin: ceramics, Vallauris, France, c. 1947.

- Throw a bottle, a vase or a jug on the wheel.
- Close the neck, or pinch out ears and a beak.
- Press the belly and add arms or legs of slip.
- Draw the face with a knife or a brush.

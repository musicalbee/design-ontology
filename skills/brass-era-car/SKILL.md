# Brass-Era Car

> The first car is a carriage that has lost its horse. It keeps the wood, the leather and the brass of the coach. It shows its engine as a proud machine.

The first motor cars. A tall, upright carriage body on wood-spoke wheels, with open seats, a folding hood, and lamps, radiator and horn of polished brass.

**Period:** 1886–1918 · **Era:** Victorian dress · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Veteran car, Edwardian motor car, Horseless carriage

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Brass-Era Car, you MUST:

1. Set the body high on the chassis, with the seat 90 to 110 cm above the road.
2. Build the body on an ash frame, and clad it in thin sheet metal or in wood panels.
3. Fit wood-spoke wheels, 12 to 16 spokes each, with a thin rim and a narrow tyre.
4. Polish the lamps, the radiator shell, the horn and the door handles in brass, and keep them bright.
5. Fix the hood as a folding canvas frame on iron bows, with leather straps.
6. Stuff the seats deep with horsehair, and button the leather in a diamond pattern.
7. Leave the engine, the chain or shaft and the springs in view.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A tall, narrow box on high wheels, with an upright seat, a short bonnet and a flat floor; the body looks like a carriage.
- **Line:** Vertical lines and a flat top; the bonnet is short and level, and the mudguard is a thin curve over each wheel.
- **Fit:** Open to the weather: two rows of seats, a folding hood and a windscreen that folds flat.
- **Proportion:** Wheel diameter 80 to 100 cm; wheelbase 2.2 to 3 m; body height about 1.8 m with the hood up.
- **Rules:**
  - Place the driver high and upright, with the hands near the chest.
  - Mount the lamps on brackets at the side of the windscreen, and the horn at the driver's hand.
  - Keep the bonnet short and straight, with the brass radiator at its front.
  - Curve each mudguard thinly over the wheel, with a step board below the door.

- **Avoid:** chrome-plate · abs-plastic · fibreglass · pvc-leathercloth · carbon-fibre

## Construction

- **Methods:** ash frame joined by hand · thin panels beaten over a wood buck · brass lamps and radiator shell hand polished · canvas hood on iron bows · wood wheels with iron tyre bands · buttoned and stuffed leather seats
- **Closures:** Leather straps and brass buckles on the hood; side doors with a simple brass latch, or no door at all.
- **Details:** Brass carriage lamps at the side, a bulb horn, a brass radiator cap and a plain wood dashboard with a clock and an oil gauge.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** polished · gloss-paint · burnished · quilted · engraved · gilded
- **Pattern:** Plain body colour with a hand-painted coach line in a second colour on the panels and the wheels.
- **Gloss:** Coach varnish over paint at 70–90 GU; polished brass at more than 90 GU.
- **Wear:** Brass tarnishes in days, and the owner polishes it again; leather cracks and darkens; the varnish crazes with age.

### Coachbuilding (skill)

The making of a car body by hand on a wood frame, and the fit of that body to a chassis that the client bought. Origin: fashion, Europe, c. 1600.

- Build the body frame from ash timber and join it with metal brackets.
- Beat the aluminium or steel panels over a wood form and fit them to the frame.
- Fit the trim, the upholstery and the brightwork, then paint the body in many coats.

# Victorian architectural terracotta

> A smoky industrial city needs a facade that rain can wash: a glazed or hard-fired clay block gives rich ornament at a price that a mason cannot meet.

Fired clay blocks in buff, red and blue-grey that English makers pressed in moulds for museums, schools and town halls. Cheap in a smoky city, the clay kept its colour where stone turned black.

**Period:** 1860–1900 · **Era:** Industrial pottery · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Victorian terracotta, Gothic Revival terracotta, Press-moulded terracotta facade

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Victorian architectural terracotta, you MUST:

1. Press the ornament in plaster moulds, so that one design repeats across a whole facade.
2. Make each block hollow, with webs inside, 25 to 50 mm thick, so that it dries and fires without a crack.
3. Use one colour for the main wall and a second for the band, the arch and the frieze.
4. Model plant and animal forms in high relief: leaves, birds, fruit, beasts and Romanesque arches.
5. Fire the blocks at a high heat until the clay is hard and does not take soot.
6. Hang the blocks on a brick or iron frame with iron cramps and fill the cavity with mortar.
7. Add a glaze or a coloured slip for the bands that need to wash clean in the rain.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Facade blocks, string courses, window surrounds, columns, capitals, finials, chimney pots and big relief panels for friezes.
- **Profile:** A deep relief with a sharp edge and a strong shadow; arches are round or pointed, and the leaves stand out 3 to 8 cm from the ground.
- **Foot and rim:** Each block is hollow and open at the back; the face has a sharp arris and the edges are straight so that the joints stay thin.
- **Proportion:** A block is about 30 to 60 cm long and 15 to 30 cm high; the wall of the block is 25 to 50 mm thick.
- **Rules:** Make each block hollow with internal webs. · Repeat the mould for the same motif. · Use two clay colours in bands. · Give each block a number for its place on the plan.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** terracotta
- **Secondary:** fired-brick
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · jasperware · parian · paper-clay
- **Rule:** Blend several clays and a grog of ground fired clay, so that the body presses cleanly and fires hard with little shrinkage. Keep the colour even in each batch.

## Forming

- **Methods:** pressing in plaster moulds · hand finishing of the undercuts · drying in a warm shed · firing in a muffle kiln
- **Joins:** Blocks are bedded in lime or cement mortar on a brick backing and tied with iron cramps; joints are 3 to 6 mm thin.
- **Details:** A mould seam on the back, a maker stamp in the body, and a number or letter that marks the place of the block.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** bare-clay · majolica-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** relief-moulding · incised · undecorated
- **Pattern:** Rows of leaves, flowers, birds and beasts in high relief; bands of tiles and plain panels in buff and red, with a blue-grey for the arch.
- **Gloss:** Matt for the bare body, about 5 to 20 GU; glazed bands reach 60 to 85 GU.
- **Wear:** The bare body keeps its edge but takes a dark patina in a city; glazed parts wash clean in the rain.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Warm buff, brick red and a cool blue-grey in large flat fields; a few glazed bands in turquoise, green or cream give a bright accent.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A muffle or downdraught kiln fired with coal; the blocks stand on edge in saggars, so that the flame does not touch the face.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** pressing in plaster moulds · drying for one to two weeks · a firing at about 1100 °C · a second glaze firing for glazed parts
- **Scale:** Large works in Lambeth, Leeds and Stoke supplied whole museums, schools, banks and town halls; one building used thousands of blocks.
- **Signs:** A maker stamp on the back, a thin mould seam and a slight colour change between kiln loads.

### Hollow terracotta block (process)

A building block of clay that has webs inside. A maker presses it in a mould, so that it dries and fires without a crack and weighs little. Origin: ceramics, England, c. 1850.

- Press the clay in a plaster mould to the shape and the relief of the block.
- Leave the back open and add webs inside, with a wall of 25 to 50 mm.
- Dry the block slowly, then fire it hard at about 1100 °C.
- Bed the block in mortar and tie it to the backing wall or the steel frame.

# Delftware

> A cheap local clay looks like Chinese porcelain when a white tin glaze covers it and a fast brush paints it in cobalt blue.

Tin-glazed earthenware that the potters of Delft made after 1640 to copy Chinese blue-and-white porcelain. A thin buff body takes a thick white tin glaze and cobalt painting in one firing.

**Period:** 1640–1780 · **Era:** The porcelain race · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Delft blue, Dutch tin-glazed earthenware, Hollandse porselein

- the brief is for oven or dishwasher ware

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Delftware, you MUST:

1. Cover the whole buff body with an opaque white tin glaze 0.5 to 1 mm thick.
2. Paint the cobalt on the raw, dry glaze in one pass; the glaze takes the stroke at once.
3. Divide a plate rim into 6 or 8 panels after Chinese kraak porcelain.
4. Put one scene in the well of the plate: a landscape, a flower vase or Chinese figures.
5. Give a fine piece a clear lead overglaze, the kwaart, for a higher gloss.
6. Fire the glaze at 1000 to 1100 °C in saggars, so that the flame does not touch the glaze.
7. Mark the base with the sign of the pottery and the initials of the owner.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Flat plates and chargers with a wide rim, lobed dishes, tall vases and tulip vases with spouts, and jugs with a pewter lid.
- **Profile:** A shallow well that rises to a flat or slightly curved rim; vases have a round belly and a long neck after Chinese models.
- **Foot and rim:** A low foot ring or a flat base with holes for a hanging cord; the rim is plain or lobed, and the edge shows small chips of glaze.
- **Proportion:** Plate diameter 20 to 35 cm and wall 5 to 8 mm thick, which is thicker than the porcelain that it copies.
- **Rules:**
  - Copy the shape of the Chinese model and keep the earthenware wall thicker.
  - Throw round ware on the wheel and press lobed dishes in moulds.
  - Keep the back of a plate glazed white, often with a thinner lead glaze.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** earthenware
- **Secondary:** silver
- **Avoid:** porcelain · bone-china · stoneware
- **Rule:** Mix three local and imported marls into a buff earthenware that fires soft and takes a thick tin glaze. Keep porcelain out: delftware copies it.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the kick wheel · press moulding in plaster or clay moulds · turning the foot when leather-hard · slab-built tiles and plaques
- **Joins:** Handles and spouts are pulled or moulded and luted with slip; metal lids are mounted after the firing.
- **Details:** Three small marks of the kiln stilts under the rim, a slightly uneven glaze edge, and a pottery mark painted under the base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** tin-glaze · lead-glaze
- **Decorations:** underglaze-cobalt · overglaze-enamel · gilding
- **Pattern:** Chinese landscapes, flower vases, birds on branches, Dutch windmills and biblical scenes in cobalt blue on white, in panels after kraak porcelain.
- **Gloss:** Satin to gloss, about 40 to 70 GU; pieces with a kwaart overglaze are brighter.
- **Wear:** The tin glaze chips at the rim and shows the buff body under it; the colour of the painting stays fresh.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Cobalt blue on a milk-white ground; after 1700 some pieces add manganese purple, iron red and a little gold.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A wood-fired updraught kiln with saggars; one firing for the biscuit and one for the glaze.
- **Atmosphere:** oxidation
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 980 °C · dipping in tin glaze · painting on the raw glaze · kwaart overglaze on fine pieces · glaze firing in saggars
- **Scale:** Some 30 potteries in Delft in the 1660s to 1720s, each with tens of painters on piece work.
- **Signs:** A pottery mark under the base, stilt marks on the back, and a slight blue blur where the cobalt sank into the glaze.

### Pounce transfer (process)

A drawing on paper is pricked along its lines and dabbed with a bag of charcoal dust, so that dots of the design go onto the surface below. Origin: painting, Florence, Italy, c. 1400.

- Draw the design at full size on paper.
- Prick holes along each line, 2 to 3 mm apart.
- Lay the sheet on the surface and dab it with a bag of charcoal or graphite dust.
- Paint over the dots; the dust burns away in the kiln or wipes off.

# Bandeau Brassiere

> The body needs a light band, not a cage: a soft brassiere carries the bust and leaves the waist free for the new, loose dress.

The first modern brassiere: a light, unboned band or two cups of silk, cotton and lace, tied at the back. It replaced the corset above the waist and then flattened the bust for the 1920s dress.

**Period:** 1914–1929 · **Era:** Belle Époque and the Great War · **Domain:** Fashion · **Also called:** Backless brassière, Flapper bandeau, Early brassiere

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting dress and what goes with it: garments, outdoor wear, underwear, shoes, accessories, jewellery, watches and the styling of cars in Bandeau Brassiere, you MUST:

1. Make the brassiere of two small panels joined at the centre front by a short strip.
2. Leave out all bone and steel; hold the shape with the cut and with a tape.
3. Tie or hook the band at the back, and set it low enough to leave the back bare.
4. Cut the band 12 to 20 cm deep, so that it covers the bust and no more.
5. Make the body in fine cotton, silk or crêpe de Chine, and trim the edge with lace.
6. Fasten the straps with narrow ribbon, tied at the shoulder.
7. From 1920, cut the band flat to press the bust level with the chest.

## Silhouette & form

- **Shape:** A short, flat band across the chest, with narrow straps, over a plain, straight torso.
- **Line:** A level line across the chest; there is no waist and no bust point.
- **Fit:** Light and close at the chest; the tape at the back is adjustable.
- **Proportion:** Band 12 to 20 cm deep; straps 1 to 2 cm wide; centre front 4 to 8 cm.
- **Rules:**
  - Cut two panels, and join them at the centre front with a short seam.
  - Add an elastic or a plain tape at the back and tie it at the side.
  - Edge the top and the bottom with a narrow lace or a bias strip.
  - Keep the back low, to fit the dress with a bare back.
  - Wear it with a chemise or a loose slip, and no corset.

- **Primary:** cambric · crepe-de-chine · satin

## Construction

- **Methods:** two panels cut from a rectangle · centre front seam · narrow tape at the back · bias-cut strip edges · ribbon straps tied at the shoulder · lace trim at the top
- **Closures:** A tie or a hook at the back, and ribbon straps; later versions use a small metal hook and eye.
- **Details:** A narrow lace at the top edge, a small ribbon rose at the centre, and elastic at the back in the late 1920s.

## Surface & finish

- **Finishes:** matte · glazed · embroidered · mercerised
- **Pattern:** Plain white, ivory or blush; for evening, lace or small embroidery along the edge.
- **Gloss:** Cotton and crêpe de Chine at 3–10 GU; satin at 30–55 GU.
- **Wear:** A washed brassiere softens and loses its colour; the tape at the back stretches first.

- **Processes:** paper pattern cut in few sizes · panels sewn on a machine · bias strips cut and sewn at the edge · lace sewn on by hand · tape and ribbon fitted at the back

### Corselet-gorge (skill)

A garment that holds the bust on its own, apart from the corset below it, so that the weight rests on the shoulders and not on the waist. Origin: fashion, Paris, 1889.

- Cut the support as a separate piece with two cups and a back band.
- Hang the cups from two shoulder straps.
- Fasten the band at the back with hooks or ties.

## Media

The salons, schools, magazines, publishers, galleries and platforms that carried this style.

- **Maidenform** (workshop, Bayonne, New Jersey, 1922–now), made the style. A maker of brassières founded in 1922 for the flapper years. It was run by Ida Rosenthal, and in the Second World War it turned its factories to pigeon vests and parachutes.

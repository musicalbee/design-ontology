# Chef studio tableware

> A chef and a potter design the plate with the dish: a matt, earthy and slightly uneven vessel gives the food a ground that no factory plate can give.

Hand-made stoneware plates and bowls that a studio potter makes with a chef for one restaurant. The pieces have an uneven rim, a matt or earth-toned glaze and a bare clay foot, and they frame the food like a landscape.

**Period:** 2005–present · **Era:** Plural and contemporary · **Domain:** Ceramics · **Also called:** Restaurant ceramics, New Nordic stoneware, Hand-thrown plates for chefs

## System directives

When specifying, sourcing or prompting fired clay: tableware, teaware, kitchen and storage ware, vases and display vessels, figures, tiles, architectural ceramics and sanitary and technical ware in Chef studio tableware, you MUST:

1. Throw each piece by hand on the wheel, and leave a trace of the throwing rings.
2. Make the rim a little uneven, and the well wide, so that the food sits like a landscape.
3. Glaze in a matt or ash glaze in an earth colour: stone, moss, rust, bone or charcoal.
4. Leave a bare clay foot and a bare patch where the piece stood in the kiln.
5. Make a new shape for each dish, in a run of 20 to 200 pieces.
6. Let the glaze move in the kiln: it pools, breaks at the edge and shows the body.
7. Fire in reduction at 1250 °C so that the iron shows in the glaze.

## Form & profile

- **Shape:** Wide, shallow plates with an uneven rim, deep bowls with a thick lip, slab platters, small tasting dishes and cups without a handle.
- **Profile:** A wide shallow well and a low, soft rim, or a deep bowl that curves up to a thick lip; the line is not exact.
- **Foot and rim:** A bare, turned foot ring with a rough ring of body; the rim is a soft, slightly uneven edge.
- **Proportion:** Plate diameter 20 to 32 cm, wall 4 to 8 mm thick; the well is wide and the rim is only about 15 per cent of the width.
- **Rules:** Keep the well wide, so that the food is the main shape. · Leave a small irregularity in the rim and the foot. · Make the piece for one dish, and test it with that dish.

## Clay bodies

- **Primary:** stoneware · porcelain
- **Secondary:** red-stoneware
- **Avoid:** bone-china · creamware · vitreous-china
- **Rule:** Use a stoneware with some grog or iron that shows through the glaze. Use porcelain for a fine small plate. Do not use factory bone china: the style is hand-made.

## Forming

- **Methods:** throwing on the electric wheel · turning the foot when leather-hard · hand building of slab platters · brushing and dipping of glaze · wax resist on the foot · mending with lacquer and gold, in the manner of kintsugi
- **Joins:** A cup has no handle; a lid, when it is needed, fits a rebated seat that the potter checks by hand.
- **Details:** Throwing rings on the wall, finger marks at the rim, a glaze that thins at the edge, and a stamp or a mark in the base.

## Glaze & decoration

- **Glazes:** matte-glaze · ash-glaze · shino-glaze · satin-glaze · bare-clay
- **Decorations:** undecorated · incised · wax-resist · kintsugi
- **Pattern:** No pattern; a bare band, a wax-resist ring, a fine incised line, or a glaze that runs in a soft pool.
- **Gloss:** Matt to satin, about 10 to 45 GU.
- **Wear:** The matt glaze shows cutlery marks, and the bare clay takes colour from food; makers regard this as part of the piece.

## Palette

- **Strategy:** Earth tones in a matt finish: stone, moss, rust, bone and charcoal, with one pale colour for contrast.

## Firing

- **Kiln:** A small gas or electric kiln, sometimes a wood or soda kiln, fired in reduction.
- **Atmosphere:** reduction
- **Processes:** biscuit firing at about 950 °C · glaze dipping or brushing · wax resist on the foot · glaze firing in reduction at about 1250 °C · sorting and numbering of the run
- **Scale:** A studio of 1 to 10 people that makes runs of 20 to 200 pieces for one restaurant.
- **Signs:** Throwing rings, a glaze that varies from piece to piece, a bare foot, and the stamp of the studio.

### Ash glazing (material)

A glaze that comes from wood or plant ash. The ash melts with the clay and makes a green, yellow or brown glass. Origin: ceramics, China (Shang dynasty), c. 1500 BCE.

- Burn wood or straw and sieve the ash.
- Wash the ash to take out the soluble salts.
- Mix it with clay and feldspar, or use it alone.
- Pour or dip the glaze and fire to 1250 °C or more.
